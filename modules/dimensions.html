Module 10  ·  Dimension reduction

What counts as a dimension

Every module so far has treated an attribute as a dimension, because that is the usual arrangement and because it had to be something. It is a choice, and there are others. A window of twenty-eight time samples is twenty-eight dimensions. A magnitude spectrum of twelve frequencies is twelve. The same arithmetic runs on all three, the eigenvectors come back looking completely different in each, and on this survey the choice mattered more than the choice of method did.

1One trace, written down three ways

A dimension is whatever you decided to put on an axis. Nothing in the arithmetic knows or cares what that was.

Switch the feature space in the header. Same trace, same window, three different lists of numbers describing it.

The longer version

Six attributes computed over the window make a six-dimensional vector. The twenty-eight time samples in that same window make a twenty-eight-dimensional one. The twelve frequency magnitudes of the same window make a twelve-dimensional one. All three describe the same piece of seismic data and all three are complete enough to work with.

Every trace in the survey supplies one of these vectors, so each feature space gives a cloud of 5184 points in a space of its own. Everything from module 01 onward applies to each of them identically, because nothing in a covariance matrix knows what its axes were.

2When a time sample is a dimension, the eigenvectors are wavelets

Step through the components

Drag through the components. These are eigenvectors, the same object as module 04's loadings — and here each one is a waveform with 3, 3, 4 and 4 zero crossings.

The longer version

A loading is one number per dimension. When the dimensions are the twenty-eight time samples of a window, a loading vector has twenty-eight numbers laid out in time, which is a wavelet. Nothing made them wavelet-shaped except the data: the traces are wavelets, so the directions of greatest spread among them are wavelets too.

The score for each component is then how much of that particular waveform is present on each trace, which is a map. This is what AASPI's waveform classification programs compute, and it is where their documentation calls the eigenvectors the initial training waveforms and the projections the principal components.

The first four hold 55.5%, 21.5%, 11.2% and 7.4% of the variance, so four of twenty-eight dimensions reach 95.5% — a much harder compression than six attributes managed.

3When a frequency is a dimension, they are spectra

Component 2 is the one to look at

Component 1 is all one sign — a brightness measure, peaking at 39 Hz. Component 2 changes sign: low frequencies against high. That one is a frequency shift, and it separates the channel at 8.46.

The longer version

Two components reach 95.6% here, against four for the waveform space and four for the attributes. Magnitude spectra of a band-limited wavelet are smooth and strongly redundant between neighboring frequencies, so there is very little independent information across twelve bins.

A component whose loadings are all the same sign is measuring how much there is of everything, which is an amplitude. A component whose loadings change sign is measuring a balance between the parts on either side of the change, which for a spectrum is a frequency shift. The channel here is a tuned thin bed, so it shifts the spectrum rather than simply brightening it, and the sign-changing component is the one that finds it.

Guo, Marfurt and Liu (2009) set this out as principal component spectral analysis. The AASPI implementation goes further and decomposes magnitude and phase together as a single complex quantity, which this module does not do.

4The feature space mattered more than the method

Fill the bar

Find the feature space and component that shows the channel best. Then compare what you find against everything the previous nine modules achieved.

The longer version

On the attribute space, the best principal component separated the channel at 3.11 and the best independent component at 6.30 — module 08's headline, and a good argument for the harder method.

On the spectral space, ordinary PCA reaches 8.46. Changing what counted as a dimension did more than changing the method did, on the same window of the same survey, and it cost nothing beyond a different way of writing the data down.

That is not an argument that spectra are better than attributes. It is an argument that the choice of feature space is a decision with consequences, and it is normally made without being noticed at all.

5Choosing a feature space on purpose

Three questions, three answers

The feature space is chosen by what you are looking for, and the programs that implement each one are separate programs with separate documentation.

The three feature spaces as properties

Measured here, except where noted

Show

None of the three is a different method. Every number on this page came out of the same eigensolver reading the same kind of covariance matrix, and the only thing that changed between them was what got written on the axes.

Why it matters

Where this shows up in real work

The feature space is chosen before anything else and is usually inherited from whichever program was opened. It is worth choosing on purpose.

  • Reading a paper or a manual. "PCA on seismic data" describes at least three different analyzes. A figure of eigenvectors that look like wavelets is the waveform space; a bar chart against attribute names is the attribute space. They are not comparable.
  • Waveform classification. Projecting traces onto a few eigen-wavelets and classifying the result is a standard facies workflow, and the eigen-wavelets are the vocabulary it uses.
  • Before reaching for a harder method. Here, rewriting the data as spectra outperformed switching from PCA to ICA. That is a cheap thing to try first.
  • Knowing what the components mean. All-one-sign loadings measure how much there is of everything. Sign-changing loadings measure a balance. That reading works in every feature space, and it is usually the fastest way to name a component.

Two points that are commonly confused. The first is that more dimensions means more information. The spectral feature space has twelve dimensions carrying less independent content than six attributes did — two components reach 95% — because neighboring frequencies of a band-limited wavelet are nearly the same measurement. The second is that these are alternative methods. They are one method applied to three different descriptions of identical data.

The result on this page is one target on one synthetic survey. What transfers is not that spectra win, but that the choice of feature space is a lever most people never touch.

Exercises

  1. Count the dimensions three ways. Why: "dimension" is the word that causes the most trouble, and this fixes it. In step 1, switch between the three feature spaces and note how many numbers describe one trace in each.
    HintSix, twenty-eight and twelve, for the same window of the same trace. The survey has 5184 traces, so each feature space gives a cloud of 5184 points in a space of that many dimensions. Nothing in the arithmetic notices the difference: a covariance matrix is a covariance matrix whether its axes are attributes, milliseconds or hertz.
  2. Look at an eigenvector that is a wavelet. Why: seeing a loading vector as a signal rather than a bar chart is what makes the waveform literature readable. In step 2, step through the first four components and count the zero crossings of each.
    HintThree, three, four and four. They look like wavelets because the data are wavelets — the direction of greatest spread among a set of wavelets is itself a wavelet. The four components hold 55.0%, 21.6%, 11.5% and 7.4%, reaching 95.6% between them, so four of twenty-eight dimensions carry almost everything. The score of each is a map of how much of that particular waveform sits on each trace, which is exactly what a waveform classification is built from.
  3. Name a component from its loadings alone. Why: this reading works in every feature space and is the quickest way to know what a component contains. In step 3, compare the sign pattern of spectral component 1 against component 2, then look at their maps.
    HintComponent 1 is positive at every frequency, peaking at 39 Hz, so it measures how much spectrum there is altogether — a brightness. Component 2 runs 0.47, 0.54, 0.32 at the low end and −0.37, −0.41, −0.26 in the middle, so it measures low frequencies against high, which is a frequency shift. The channel is a tuned thin bed and shifts the spectrum rather than simply brightening it, so component 2 is the one that finds it: 8.46 against 2.16. All-one-sign means an amount, sign-changing means a balance, and that holds for attribute loadings and eigen-wavelets equally.
  4. Beat module 08 without changing the method. Why: the feature space is a cheaper lever than the algorithm and almost nobody reaches for it. In step 4, find the best component of each feature space and set the three against the results marked on the panel. Before starting: module 08 needed ICA to get from 3.11 to 6.30. Commit to whether ordinary PCA in a different feature space can do better.
    HintAttributes: 3.11. Time samples: 3.50. Frequencies: 8.46. Plain PCA on the spectral space beats the independent component analysis of module 08, which reached 6.30, and beats every attribute result in the set. Nothing about the method changed — same eigensolver, same covariance matrix, same survey, same window. Only the axes were relabeled. Two readings of that are available and the page does not choose between them. It may be that spectra suit this target, because a tuned thin bed shifts the spectrum and the attribute set contains only one frequency measure. Or it may be that twelve frequency bins describe the window more completely than six hand-chosen attributes do. The experiment run here cannot separate those. What it does establish is that changing the feature space is worth trying before reaching for a harder algorithm.
  5. Find where the extra dimensions went. Why: a bigger space is not automatically a richer one, and this is the clearest case in the set. Read how many components each feature space needs to reach 90% of its variance.
    HintAttributes need four of six. Time samples need four of twenty-eight. Frequencies need two of twelve. The spectral feature space has twice as many dimensions as the attribute space and less independent content in them, because the magnitude spectrum of a band-limited wavelet is smooth and neighboring bins are nearly the same number. Counting dimensions tells you about the description; counting components tells you about the data.

If you remember nothing else

  • A dimension is whatever you put on an axis. An attribute, a millisecond, a hertz. The arithmetic does not know the difference.
  • Eigenvectors look like whatever the axes were. Twenty-eight time samples give eigenvectors that are wavelets, with 3, 3, 4 and 4 zero crossings here.
  • All-one-sign loadings measure an amount; sign-changing loadings measure a balance. That reading names a component in any feature space.
  • The feature space can matter more than the method. Plain PCA on spectra reached 8.46 against 6.30 for ICA on attributes, on the same window.
  • More dimensions is not more information. Twelve frequencies needed two components for 90%; six attributes needed four.
  • These are not three methods. One method, three descriptions of identical data.

Method

What is computed, and what is left out

All three feature spaces use the same window, samples 22 to 50 of the same 72 by 72 trace synthetic survey the whole set uses, and the same 5184 traces.

The attribute feature space is the six of modules 04 to 08, Z-scored, which is the correlation route of module 03. The waveform feature space takes the twenty-eight samples directly with means removed and no standardization, because every sample is already in the same amplitude units and standardizing them individually would distort the wavelet shape. The spectral feature space takes the magnitude spectrum of the Hann-tapered window on a 64-point transform, keeping the first twelve bins, which at 2 ms sampling is 7.8 to 93.8 Hz, again with means removed only.

The separation statistic is identical to modules 05 to 09 and is described in module 06's Method tab, so every number here can be set directly beside the ones there. The comparison figures quoted from earlier modules are 3.11 for the best attribute principal component, 6.30 for the best independent component and 9.34 for the best direction module 06 found, all on the attribute feature space.

What this leaves out of the published methods. AASPI's complex principal component spectral analysis decomposes magnitude and phase together as one complex quantity with a self-adjoint covariance matrix, following Guo et al. (2009); this module uses magnitudes only, which is simpler and discards the phase. Its waveform programs run on windows tracked along an interpreted horizon rather than on a flat window, and classify the resulting scores rather than stopping at them. Neither difference changes what an eigenvector is or what it looks like, which is what this module is about.

An error in the source documentation, for the record. The AASPI documents for pca_waveform_classification and real_pca_waveform state that the principal components are not orthogonal. Module 04 measures the score vectors of this set at mutual dot products of about 10⁻¹⁵, which is orthogonal to machine precision; with the means removed, uncorrelated and orthogonal are the same statement. The two documents also have their menu paths swapped, and complex_pca_spectra writes the eigenvalue decomposition of the covariance matrix where it means the eigenvector matrix.

Simplifications, stated plainly. One window on one synthetic survey. The window is flat, where real waveform work follows a horizon and a flat window across dipping strata mixes different geology into the same dimension. No decimation. The spectral feature space keeps twelve bins because that covers the useful band of a 30 Hz wavelet; a different choice of transform length or band would give different numbers.
