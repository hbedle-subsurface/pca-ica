Module 07  ·  Dimension reduction

Independence is not decorrelation

Module 06 ended with component scores that are uncorrelated to fifteen decimal places and distributions that are visibly not Gaussian. Those two facts are compatible, and the gap between them is where a second family of methods lives. These steps establish what dependence without correlation looks like, what whitening does, what statistic can see the difference, and why a mixture of things always looks blander than the things that went into it.

1Zero correlation, and knowing one still tells you about the other

Read the bin spreads under the cloud

Why this step is here. Correlation is so often used as a synonym for relatedness that the difference has to be met head-on before anything built on it makes sense.

The correlation is zero to fifteen decimal places. Now read the heights of the bars below.

The longer version

Two quantities are uncorrelated when knowing one tells you nothing about the average of the other. They are independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the other at all, including its spread, its shape and its tails. The second is a much stronger statement and correlation cannot test it.

The second and third component scores are uncorrelated at about ten to the minus fifteen, which is as decorrelated as arithmetic gets. Split the traces into bins by their value of the first and measure the spread of the second within each bin, as a fraction of its own overall spread, and the answer runs from 0.56 to 2.36 — a factor of more than four. Knowing where a trace sits on one component predicts how far it can stray on the other. That is dependence, and the correlation coefficient is blind to it.

The outer bins hold few traces and their spreads are noisy, which is why the counts are drawn alongside. The pattern across the well-populated middle bins is the one to read.

2Whitening takes variance out of the argument

Switch the coordinates in the header

Press Whitened in the header. The curve goes flat at 1.0000 and stays flat at every angle.

The longer version

Whitening projects onto the components and then divides each projection by the square root of its eigenvalue. Every direction then carries a variance of exactly one, the cloud becomes a round ball, and the covariance matrix becomes the identity.

The curve below is the variance along a direction as that direction turns through the plane of the two chosen components. Before whitening it has a maximum and a minimum, and finding the maximum is the whole of what PCA did. After whitening it is flat at 1.0000 to four decimal places at every angle.

That flatness is the point. Variance can no longer prefer one direction over another, so a method that only knows about variance has nothing left to say. Anything that goes further has to be looking at something else.

Variance along a direction, before and after whitening

Same axis, same scale

Coordinates

Whitening is reversible and throws nothing away. It is a change of coordinates, not a filter. What it removes is the ability of second-order statistics to distinguish directions, which is exactly the ability that has already been used up.

3Putting a number on the shape

Three reference distributions, then the real ones

Compare the fourth panel against the first three. Uniform is −1.2, Gaussian is 0, Laplace is 3. That is the scale.

The longer version

Mean and variance are first and second order: where a distribution sits and how wide it is. Neither says anything about its shape, and two distributions with identical means and variances can look nothing alike.

Excess kurtosis is a fourth-order statistic. It is exactly zero for a Gaussian, negative for a flat-topped distribution and positive for one with a sharp peak and long tails. A uniform distribution gives −1.2 exactly and a Laplace gives +3 exactly, which fixes the scale. It is cheap to compute and sensitive to outliers, because a fourth power gives one extreme sample a great deal of influence.

Negentropy measures how far a distribution sits from the Gaussian of the same variance. It is never negative and is exactly zero only for a Gaussian, which makes it the cleaner quantity of the two, and it cannot be computed exactly from a finite sample, so implementations use an approximation built from one or two simple functions.

Three references, and a component score beside them

All four scaled to the same variance
Sharply peaked with long tails is what a map made mostly of background with a few bright features looks like when its values are collected into a histogram. Most traces sit near the middle and a small number sit a long way out, which is the shape a sparse geological feature produces and the shape a Gaussian background does not.

4Add things together and the shape washes out

Slide the number of sources

Slide the number of sources from 1 to 8. A flat box becomes a bell by about four.

The longer version

Adding independent quantities together moves the result toward a Gaussian. That is the central limit theorem, and it is the reason the Gaussian turns up everywhere, but it is also the mechanism the next module exploits.

The panel below sums independent uniform sources. One on its own has an excess kurtosis of −1.2006 against a theoretical −1.2. Two give −0.5895, three −0.3937, four −0.2934, eight −0.1513. The measured values follow −1.2 divided by the number of sources closely, and the histogram goes from a flat box to a bell within four or five terms.

Turn that around. A measurement that looks Gaussian is what a mixture of several things looks like, and a measurement that looks strongly non-Gaussian is more likely to be one thing on its own. So among all the ways of combining a set of attributes, the combinations that look least Gaussian are the candidates for being single effects rather than mixtures. That sentence is the whole idea of independent component analysis.

Why it matters

Where this shows up in real work

Nothing in this module is a method. It establishes that there is something left to find after PCA has finished, and what kind of quantity can find it.

  • Reading a crossplot of components. A funnel, a cross, a clustered pattern — any of these is dependence, and every one of them coexists with a correlation of zero. The correlation matrix that was checked before the run says nothing about them.
  • Knowing why whitening appears in every ICA workflow. It is not a cleanup step. It is what makes the remaining search a search over rotations, which is a much smaller problem than a search over all linear transformations.
  • Judging a component by its histogram. A component whose distribution is sharply peaked with long tails is picking out something sparse. A component whose distribution is a clean bell is more likely to be several things averaged together, or noise.
  • Understanding the assumption being made. Non-Gaussianity as a proxy for being a single effect is an assumption, and it holds because sums go Gaussian. Where the underlying effects are themselves Gaussian it fails completely, which module 08 demonstrates.

Two points that are commonly confused. The first is that uncorrelated implies independent. It does for Gaussian variables and for almost nothing else, and the scores here are strongly non-Gaussian. The second is that kurtosis and negentropy are interchangeable. They rank the same way on clean data, and kurtosis involves a fourth power, so a handful of bad traces can move it a long way while negentropy barely notices.

The measured result in step 5 — an unsupervised criterion landing within one percent of a supervised optimum — is one plane on one synthetic survey. It is an argument for trying the method, not a guarantee about any other dataset.

Exercises

  1. Find dependence in a pair with zero correlation. Why: this distinction is the foundation of everything in the ICA half of the set, and it is counterintuitive until it has been seen once. With components 2 and 3 selected, read the correlation and then the spread of the vertical score within each bin of the horizontal one. Before looking: the correlation is zero to fifteen decimal places. Commit to whether you expect the bin spreads to be equal.
    HintThe correlation reads about −2×10⁻¹⁵ and the bin spreads read 1.324, 2.364, 1.331, 0.675, 0.558 and 0.855 across the populated bins, each as a fraction of that score's own overall spread — a factor of 4.24 between the widest and the narrowest. Knowing a trace's value on component 2 tells you a great deal about how far it can stray on component 3, and nothing at all about the average it will stray from. Correlation measures only the second of those. Independence would require both to be uninformative, and a great many things in an attribute set are uncorrelated without being anywhere near independent.
  2. Whiten the pair and try to find a preferred direction. Why: this shows exactly what second-order statistics have left after PCA, and the answer is nothing. In step 2, read the variance along a direction at several angles before whitening, then switch the coordinates in the header and read them again.
    HintBefore whitening the variance runs 1.2860 at 0 degrees, 1.1491 at 30, 0.8754 at 60 and 0.7386 at 90, and the maximum at zero degrees is component 2 by definition. After whitening it reads 1.000000 at every angle tested. The cloud is a round ball and no rotation of it is distinguishable from any other by variance. Note that nothing was thrown away: whitening is a reversible change of coordinates and the same traces are still there. What was removed is the ability of a covariance matrix to tell one direction from another, and that ability had already been spent.
  3. Calibrate the shape statistics against known distributions. Why: a kurtosis of 3.7 means nothing until it is set against distributions whose values are known exactly. In step 3, read the excess kurtosis of the three reference distributions and then of each component score.
    HintThe references come out close to their exact values: a uniform distribution at −1.201 against an exact −1.2, a Gaussian at −0.013 against an exact 0, and a Laplace at 2.935 against an exact 3. The departures are the sampling error of forty thousand draws, and the Laplace is furthest off because a fourth power on a long-tailed distribution is the hardest of the three to estimate. Against that scale the component scores read 5.11, 3.73, 3.85, −0.18, 5.13 and 2.78. Five of the six are more peaked than a Laplace, which is what a map holding a quiet background and a few bright features gives. Read backwards it is weaker than it looks: a strongly peaked distribution could come from a sparse geological feature, from a handful of noisy traces, or from a processing artifact over part of the survey. High kurtosis says the values are concentrated with a long tail. It does not say what made the tail. The fourth, at −0.18, is the only one close to Gaussian, and module 05 found it was also the component carrying most of the acquisition footprint — a periodic artifact spread evenly over the survey looks a great deal more Gaussian than a channel does.
  4. Watch a mixture lose its shape. Why: the assumption behind the whole next module is that mixtures look Gaussian, and it is worth watching it happen rather than accepting it. In step 4, walk the number of sources from 1 to 8 and record the excess kurtosis at each.
    HintOne source gives −1.2006, two −0.5895, three −0.3937, four −0.2934, five −0.2380, six −0.2025, seven −0.1633 and eight −0.1513. The pattern is −1.2 divided by the number of sources, which is the exact result for a sum of independent uniform variables, and the measured values sit on it to within the sampling error. The histogram is a flat box at one source and is already hard to distinguish from a bell at four. Read backwards, this is the argument for the next module: if adding things together drives the shape toward Gaussian, then undoing a mixture means looking for the combinations that are least Gaussian.
  5. Search a plane with a criterion that never sees the answer. Why: this is the first evidence in the set that the idea works, and it can be checked against the supervised ceiling from module 06. In step 5, find the angle of maximum negentropy, then read the channel separation there, and compare it against the 7.42 that module 06 obtained with the channel positions in hand.
    HintNegentropy peaks at 40 degrees, where the excess kurtosis is 8.37. The channel separation at that angle is 7.33, against a supervised best of 7.42 inside the same plane and against 2.81 for component 2, which is where PCA left the direction. A criterion that was told nothing except "look as un-Gaussian as possible" landed within about one percent of a direction chosen while looking at the answer, and roughly two and a half times better than the component PCA ranked second. Maximum kurtosis peaks at 45 degrees rather than 40, which is worth noticing: the two criteria mostly agree and do not agree exactly, and module 08 covers which to prefer.

If you remember nothing else

  • Uncorrelated is a statement about averages. Independent is a statement about everything. Two scores here are uncorrelated at 10⁻¹⁵ and their conditional spreads differ by a factor of four.
  • Whitening makes every direction carry the same variance. It is reversible, discards nothing, and leaves second-order statistics with no way to prefer one rotation.
  • Excess kurtosis is a number for shape. Zero for a Gaussian, −1.2 for a uniform, +3 for a Laplace. The component scores here run from −0.18 to 5.13.
  • Negentropy is distance from Gaussian. Never negative, zero only for a Gaussian, and more robust to a few bad traces than kurtosis is.
  • Sums go Gaussian. The kurtosis of a sum of independent uniform sources is −1.2 divided by their number, and the shape is gone by four terms.
  • So the least Gaussian combination is the best candidate for a single effect. In one plane here, maximizing that got within one percent of the supervised optimum without being told anything.

Method

What is computed, and what is left out

The survey, the six attributes and the standardization are those of modules 04 to 06. The component scores are the ones module 04 produced.

The bin spreads in step 1 divide the horizontal score into bins one standard deviation wide, from −3.5 to +3.5, and report the standard deviation of the vertical score among the traces in each. Bins holding fewer than forty traces are not drawn, because the spread of a handful of traces is not a measurement of anything. The counts are drawn alongside so that a reader can see which bins are carrying the pattern.

Whitening is carried out by assets/multivar.js: project onto the eigenvectors, then divide each projection by the square root of its eigenvalue. The result has a covariance matrix equal to the identity, which verify-multivar.js checks directly.

Excess kurtosis is the fourth central moment divided by the square of the second, minus three. Negentropy is the robust approximation of Hyvärinen and Oja (2000) using log cosh as the contrast function, computed on the standardized variable and squared, so it is small, positive, and comparable between directions but not on the same scale as an entropy in nats. Both are checked in verify-multivar.js against distributions whose values are known exactly: a uniform at −1.2, a Gaussian at 0, a sine at −1.5, and a Gaussian negentropy that must come out near zero.

The reference distributions in step 3 are drawn from a seeded generator, forty thousand samples each, so the page shows the same picture every time it is opened. A uniform is taken directly, a Gaussian by the Box-Muller transform, and a Laplace by taking the logarithm of the ratio of two uniforms. Each is standardized before display, so all four histograms have the same variance and only their shapes differ.

Step 5's plane is spanned by the whitened second and third components, and the angle is measured from the second toward the third. It is not the same parameterization as module 06's rotation, which turned through the unwhitened pair, so the angle at which the separation peaks differs between the two modules while the direction found is nearly the same one. The separation statistic is identical to module 06's and is described there.

Simplifications, stated plainly. The search in step 5 covers one plane out of a six-dimensional space, picked because module 06 established what was in it; a real search covers everything at once and has no reason to find this plane first. The negentropy approximation is one of several in use and different contrast functions peak at slightly different angles. The channel separation again uses the model's own record of where the channel is, which no real survey supplies.

Where this departs from production software. Nothing here is an algorithm, so nowhere. The one thing worth carrying forward is that implementations differ in which contrast they maximize, and the angles of maximum kurtosis and maximum negentropy in step 5 are five degrees apart on this data, which is a real if small disagreement between two defensible criteria.
