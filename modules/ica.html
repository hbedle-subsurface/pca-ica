Module 08  ·  Dimension reduction

ICA: whiten, then rotate

Module 07 searched one plane by hand and found a direction that showed the channel far better than anything principal component analysis returned. Doing that over a whole attribute space is independent component analysis. These steps run it on the same six attributes, compare what comes back against everything the set has measured so far, and go through the four things about the answer that are not determined by the data.

1Standardize, whiten, then turn until it looks least Gaussian

The six components as maps

Why this step is here. ICA is usually described by the cocktail-party story and then implemented by a fixed-point iteration nobody explains. The gap between the two is worth closing, because the parts of the answer that are undetermined follow from what the iteration actually does.

Standardize, whiten, rotate. That is the whole method. The six maps below are what comes out of it.

The longer version

The whole method is three moves. Standardize, so that attributes in unlike units are comparable, which is module 03's choice made deliberately. Whiten, so that every direction carries the same variance and second-order statistics can no longer prefer one rotation over another, which is module 07's step 2. Rotate, until each direction's projection is as far from Gaussian as it can be made.

The rotation is where the work is. For each direction in turn, the algorithm applies a fixed-point update that moves the current guess toward a nearby maximum of the contrast function, makes it perpendicular to the directions already found, and repeats until it stops moving. One direction at a time, each from what the previous ones left, which is the same deflation module 04 used for principal components — the only thing that changed is what is being maximized.

3Order, sign and scale are not in the answer

Three things the data does not fix

Step through the four starts. The same six directions come back every time, in a different order.

The longer version

Scale. Every independent component here has a variance of exactly 1.0000, because they are rotations of whitened data. There is no equivalent of an eigenvalue and no component is larger than another. Whatever scale a package writes out is a convention it chose.

Order. With no variance to rank by, the numbering is whatever order the deflation happened to produce. On this run the component with the highest negentropy is the first one, and it separates the younger channel at 0.25 — nearly nothing. The component that shows the channel is the second, and no property the algorithm knows about would have put it at the top. In the Taranaki study that introduced this workflow to seismic attributes, the components were sorted by visual inspection for exactly this reason.

Sign. As with eigenvectors, reversing a direction satisfies everything the method asked for, so the sign is a convention. This set fixes it by making the largest loading positive.

The panel below re-runs the search from four different starting rotations and matches each result against the run in the header. The directions come back, and they come back in a different order.

A deterministic start does not make the ordering meaningful. It makes it repeatable, which is a different and much weaker property: two runs of the same software on the same data agree, and the number on a component still says nothing about how useful it is.

4A component that will not converge is telling you something

Count the iterations

Read the iteration counts. One component hit the cap of 300. One finished in two.

The longer version

The fixed-point iteration stops when the direction stops moving. How long that takes varies enormously between components, and on this run it varies from one iteration to three hundred, which is the cap.

The components that converge fastest and the ones that never converge are both worth looking at. A direction whose projection is strongly non-Gaussian has a sharp maximum to climb toward and gets there quickly. A direction whose projection is close to Gaussian has nothing to climb: module 07 established that a Gaussian is exactly the case where non-Gaussianity cannot distinguish one rotation from another, so the update wanders and the iteration limit is what stops it.

On this run, component 4 reached the three-hundred-iteration cap and has an excess kurtosis of −0.29 and a negentropy of 2.6×10⁻⁵ — nearly Gaussian, as predicted. Component 6 converged in two iterations, has a negentropy of 1.8×10⁻⁶, and carries the most acquisition footprint of any component at 0.322. Neither of those is a malfunction. Both are the method reporting, in its own terms, that the direction it was handed has no structure in it to find.

5Two parameters, and what each is worth

Move both and watch the target

Find the settings that fill the bar. Ten combinations, and they do not line up the way the guidance suggests — the robust contrast does not win everywhere.

The longer version

Two things are chosen before an ICA run, and on this data they interact rather than acting separately. The second one first: how many directions to retain in the whitening step before searching. Keeping only two costs almost everything, whichever contrast is used — the best component then reaches 2.72 with log cosh and 2.69 with kurtosis, no better than PCA managed. The subspace the useful rotation lives in is simply not there to be searched.

Above three retained the two contrasts part company. Log cosh is steady, giving 6.33, 6.10, 6.18 and 6.47 at three, four, five and six. Kurtosis starts higher, at 6.60 with three retained, which is the best single figure anywhere on this page, and then falls away: 5.91, 5.48 and 4.04. The more near-Gaussian directions are included in the space being searched, the worse it does, which is the outlier sensitivity of a fourth power showing up as instability rather than as a uniformly lower number.

So the robust contrast is not better everywhere; it is less sensitive to the other choice. Log cosh varies by 0.38 across the retained counts from three up and kurtosis varies by 2.56. Picking kurtosis and getting the retained count right beats everything; picking kurtosis and getting it wrong is the worst outcome on the page. The common ninety-percent retained-variance convention selects four here, which costs about six percent with log cosh and about eleven percent with kurtosis against each one's own best.

Retaining too few directions removes the subspace the useful rotation lives in, and no choice of contrast recovers it afterward. The order of operations matters: the retained-variance decision is made by a criterion that knows only about variance, and module 06 showed that variance is not what the useful direction is large in.

Why it matters

Where this shows up in real work

An ICA run asks for a handful of parameters and returns a set of volumes with no ranking. Both halves of that are consequences of what the method is, and both change how the result has to be handled.

  • The components have to be inspected, not ranked. There is no eigenvalue, the numbering is arbitrary, and the published seismic workflow sorts by looking. Budget time for that.
  • Two runs will not agree on numbering. Across software, across parameter changes, and across a random start, IC2 in one run is not IC2 in another. Compare by pattern, not by index.
  • A non-converging component is a result. It usually means that direction is close to Gaussian, which usually means noise or a spread-out artifact. Reading the iteration count is a free diagnostic that most write-ups ignore.
  • The retained-variance choice is made before the search and constrains it. Keeping too few directions throws away the subspace the useful rotation is in, and the criterion doing the keeping only knows about variance.

Two points that are commonly confused. The first is that a fixed random seed makes the result meaningful. It makes it repeatable; the ordering still carries no information. The second is that ICA is a better version of PCA. It answers a different question, it costs an iterative search that can fail to converge, and the case where it reliably wins is the case where the inputs really are mixtures of a few non-Gaussian effects — which is an assumption about the data, not a property of the method.

Everything measured here is one synthetic survey with two channel systems and a known footprint. The comparison against a supervised ceiling is possible because the answer was built in. On a real survey the equivalent check is a well, a core, or an interpretation somebody trusts.

If you remember nothing else

  • Standardize, whiten, rotate. The first two are settled before the search starts; the search itself is only a rotation, which is what whitening buys.
  • On this survey it worked. The best independent component separates the channel at 6.47 against 2.81 for the best principal component and 5.97 for the best single attribute, reaching 72% of the supervised ceiling.
  • There is no ranking. Every component has variance exactly 1, and the component with the highest negentropy here is not the one showing the channel.
  • Order and sign are conventions. Four different starts return the same directions in a different order. A fixed start buys repeatability, not meaning.
  • A component that will not converge is nearly Gaussian. One hit the 300-iteration cap here, and one converged in a single iteration carrying the footprint.
  • The two parameters interact. Retaining two directions instead of six loses nearly everything under either contrast. Above three, log cosh varies by 0.38 and kurtosis by 2.56 — the robust contrast is the one that depends less on the other choice, not the one that always wins.

Method

What is computed, and what is left out

The implementation is the deflationary FastICA of Hyvärinen and Oja (2000), in assets/multivar.js. The data are Z-scored, whitened by projecting onto the eigenvectors and dividing each projection by the square root of its eigenvalue, and then one direction at a time is driven by the fixed-point update toward a maximum of the contrast, made orthogonal to the directions already found by Gram-Schmidt, and normalized. Convergence is declared when the direction's overlap with its previous value is within 10⁻⁹ of one; the iteration cap is 300.

The two contrasts. Log cosh uses the hyperbolic tangent as its derivative and is the robust general-purpose choice. The kurtosis contrast uses a cube, which is sharper on clean data and gives a handful of extreme samples a great deal of influence. Both are checked in verify-multivar.js against a mixture of two sources with a known mixing matrix, where both recover the sources at correlations above 0.99.

The starting rotation. By default this set starts from the identity rotation, taken one direction at a time with a small deterministic nudge off the axis so a perfectly symmetric start cannot stall. That makes re-runs byte-identical, which verify-multivar.js checks. AASPI's implementation seeds from a decorrelated version of the covariance matrix for the same reason. Step 3 uses seeded pseudo-random starts instead, to show what is and is not determined by the data.

Comparability of the numbers. The separation statistic, the footprint statistic and the target masks are identical to modules 05, 06 and 07, so every figure on this page can be set directly beside the ones there. The supervised ceiling of 8.97 for the younger channel and 1.62 for the older one comes from module 06 and is described in its Method tab.

Simplifications, stated plainly. The search runs on every one of the 5184 traces; production implementations decimate to build a smaller training set, compute the unmixing matrix on that, and apply it to the full volume, which introduces a sampling difference this page does not show. Only the deflationary form is implemented, where directions are found one at a time; the symmetric form estimates them all at once and does not privilege the order of extraction. The linear mixing assumption — that every attribute is a fixed weighted sum of a few underlying effects, with the same weights everywhere in the survey — is a strong one, and where two effects combine differently in different parts of a survey no single unmixing matrix describes it.

Where this departs from production software. The algorithm is standard and the arithmetic is the same. Implementations differ in the contrast offered, in whether the symmetric or deflationary form is used, in the convergence tolerance and cap, in whether they decimate, in the sign convention, and in whether the iteration count is reported at all. The last of those is worth asking for: step 4 shows it is diagnostic and it is usually buried in a log file.
