Module 03  ·  Dimension reduction

Units, and why they decide the answer

Coherence runs between zero and one. Spectral bandwidth runs in the tens of hertz. Amplitude runs in whatever the processor wrote out. A method that looks for the direction of greatest spread has to be told whether those are comparable quantities, and the answer it returns depends entirely on what it is told. These five steps run the same six attributes both ways and compare what comes back.

1The same six attributes, two first components

Both maps from the same survey

Why this step is here. Almost every package offers this as a checkbox, often labeled something like "normalize inputs", and it is usually left at whatever it came set to. The two settings do not produce slightly different versions of one answer. They produce two different answers.

Six attributes are computed over the same window on the same survey: RMS amplitude, peak envelope, peak frequency, spectral bandwidth, coherence and GLCM contrast. Nothing about them changes between the two routes. What changes is whether each attribute has been divided by its own standard deviation before the covariance matrix is formed.

The bars above the tabs give the loadings of the first component under each route: how much of each attribute went into it. The maps below are the resulting first component drawn back onto the survey, with the two channel axes marked on both so the same feature can be found in each.

The attribute set

Neither map is wrong. They are the answers to two different questions: which direction carries the most of the original units squared, and which direction carries the most of the shared behavior. The second question is usually the one an interpreter meant to ask, and the first is what a package computes when it is left alone.

2Multiply one attribute by a thousand

Nothing about the rocks changes

A volume written out in different units is the same volume. If a method is measuring something about the subsurface, rescaling one input should leave its answer alone. The slider in the header does exactly that rescaling, over three decades either way, and the panel below tracks what each route makes of it.

The upper panel is the loading of the first component on the attribute being rescaled, under the covariance route. The lower panel is the same quantity under the correlation route. They share one horizontal axis of gain so the two behaviors can be read straight down against each other.

What each route does as one attribute is rescaled

One shared gain axis, both fixed
The correlation route's line is flat because dividing each attribute by its own standard deviation undoes any rescaling exactly, whatever the rescaling was. That is the entire mechanism, and it is the reason the choice is usually described as making the attributes comparable rather than as making them equal.

3The covariance route is an auction

Note the axis: it runs over seven decades

The reason the covariance route behaves the way it does is visible as soon as the variances are put side by side. The upper panel gives the variance of each attribute in its own units, on a logarithmic axis because they are not otherwise comparable on one page. The lower panel gives the size of that attribute's loading on the first component under the covariance route.

The two panels have the same shape. An attribute whose numbers are large has a large variance whatever it is measuring, and the direction of greatest variance runs along it. Attributes recorded as fractions contribute a loading indistinguishable from zero, not because they carry nothing but because their numbers are small.

4With two attributes, standardizing erases the angle

Pick any pair and watch the right-hand panel

Take exactly two attributes and standardize them. The matrix that gets decomposed then has ones down its diagonal and the correlation in both off-diagonal positions, and a matrix of that shape has the same two directions regardless of what the correlation is: one at forty-five degrees and one at right angles to it. The correlation sets how long the two axes are and has no say in where they point.

The left panel below draws the chosen pair with both axes on one common numeric scale, so the angle on screen is the angle the covariance route would find in the units as given. The right panel draws the same traces standardized. Change the pair and watch: the left angle moves and the right one does not.

The same two attributes, two coordinate systems

Left in native units, right standardized
This is the reason module 01 worked in matched amplitude units rather than standardizing. Standardized, the answer to every two-attribute problem is forty-five degrees, and a student would have learned a rule that holds only because there were two attributes. With three or more the degeneracy is gone and the directions depend on the correlations again.

5What an equal share costs

Switch the seventh attribute in and out

Standardizing gives every attribute a variance of exactly one. That is what makes the attributes comparable, and it is also a statement that each of them deserves the same weight. The seventh attribute in this set is RMS amplitude measured in a window above the channel system, which is a correctly computed attribute on an interval that contains none of the target.

The panels below give the fraction of variance on each component, with and without it, and the loading it carries on each. The covariance route ignores it. The correlation route cannot, because it has already declared it equal.

Variance per component, and where the weak attribute lands

Same component axis in both panels

Which route

Neither behavior is a defect. The covariance route ignoring a weak attribute is the same mechanism that makes it ignore coherence, and the correlation route promoting one is the same mechanism that lets coherence be heard at all. What the choice cannot do is be made once and forgotten, because it depends on whether the attributes in front of you deserve equal weight.

Why it matters

Where this shows up in real work

This is the first decision in a dimension reduction and the one most often made by default. Everything downstream — which components come out, how many are worth keeping, what the RGB blend looks like — follows from it.

  • Reading someone else's components. Two groups can run the same method on the same attributes and publish different first components. Before deciding one of them is wrong, ask which route each took.
  • Mixing units in an attribute set. Any set containing both a coherence and an amplitude has a variance spread of several decades in it, and the covariance route on such a set is answering a question about the amplitude alone.
  • Attribute selection before clustering. Implementations that Z-score their inputs before whitening, which is the common arrangement for seismic ICA, have made this choice for you. It is worth knowing they have.
  • Assembling the input list. Once every attribute is guaranteed an equal share, an attribute that carries nothing about the target still occupies a dimension, and it can occupy a high-ranked one.

Two points that are commonly confused. The first is that standardizing is the neutral choice. It is a decision that every attribute matters equally, which is a strong claim about an attribute set and is sometimes false. The second is that the covariance route preserves the physics. It preserves the units, which is not the same thing: whichever attribute happens to have been written out with the largest numbers dominates, and that is a property of the file rather than of the rocks.

A third route exists and is not covered here: scaling each attribute by something other than its own standard deviation, such as a fixed expected range or a weight chosen by the interpreter. It is uncommon in seismic practice and it is the honest answer when neither of the two standard choices describes what the attributes are worth.

Exercises

  1. Read the two first components against each other. Why: this is the comparison that shows the choice is not cosmetic. With the six-attribute set and no rescaling, read the loadings of the first component under each route from the bars above the tabs. Before looking: six attributes, all computed on the same window of the same survey. How different can the two answers be? Commit to an expectation.
    HintThe covariance route returns a first component that is 0.950 spectral bandwidth, −0.274 GLCM contrast and −0.150 peak frequency, with loadings of −0.001 on RMS amplitude, −0.001 on peak envelope and 0.006 on coherence. Three of the six attributes are not in it at all. The correlation route returns 0.509 RMS, 0.379 peak envelope, 0.258 peak frequency, −0.439 bandwidth, −0.439 coherence and 0.381 GLCM contrast — all six present, and the largest loading is on an attribute the other route ignored completely. The two components share almost nothing.
  2. Rescale an attribute and watch which answer survives. Why: a method whose answer depends on the units of its inputs is measuring the file rather than the subsurface. Choose RMS amplitude in the rescale control and take the gain from 1× to 1000×, reading the covariance-route loading on it at each stop, then the correlation-route loading.
    HintUnder the covariance route the loading on RMS amplitude reads −0.001 at 1×, −0.008 at 10×, −0.083 at 100× and 0.768 at 1000×. An attribute that was invisible becomes the largest contributor to the first component, and no rock in the survey is different at any point in that slide. The share of variance the first component holds moves too, from 69.55% to 72.84%. Under the correlation route the loading sits at 0.509 and the share at 52.19% at every single stop. Note also where the change happens: nothing much moves until 100×, because the loading depends on the variance, which grows as the square of the gain — RMS amplitude has to be scaled up by about three decades before its variance is comparable with spectral bandwidth's.
  3. Look at the variances that decide it. Why: the covariance route is not arbitrary, it is predictable, and knowing what it will do is enough to know whether to use it. In step 3, read the variance of each attribute in native units off the logarithmic axis.
    HintSpectral bandwidth 74.4, GLCM contrast 23.5, peak frequency 18.1, coherence 0.0101, peak envelope 0.000207, RMS amplitude 0.000105. The largest and the smallest differ by a factor of about seven hundred thousand. The first component under the covariance route loads on those attributes in the same order, which is why it is worth predicting the answer before running it: the covariance route on a mixed-unit set returns the loudest attribute and a small correction.
  4. Standardize two attributes and look for the angle. Why: a result that is true only for two attributes is worth meeting deliberately rather than generalizing from by accident. In step 4, step through four pairs and record the native angle and the standardized angle for each.
    HintRMS with peak envelope: native 58.99 degrees, standardized 45.00. RMS with coherence: native 93.65, standardized 135.00. Peak frequency with bandwidth: native 99.45, standardized 135.00. Coherence with GLCM contrast: native 90.85, standardized 135.00. The standardized answer takes one of two values and which one depends only on the sign of the correlation. The correlation itself, anywhere from −0.72 to +0.65 across those four pairs, has no effect on the direction at all — only on how elongated the cloud is along it. Add a third attribute and the degeneracy disappears.
  5. Add an attribute that misses the target. Why: an equal share is given to every input, including the ones that should not have been in the list. Switch the seventh attribute in, with the standardized route selected in step 5, and read the variance on each component and the loading the seventh attribute carries.
    HintRMS above the target averages 0.04559 inside the young channel and 0.04660 in the background, a difference of two percent, against 0.08126 and 0.05571 for the same attribute measured on the target itself. It carries almost nothing about the channel. Under the covariance route its loading on the first component is 0.000 and the fractions of variance do not move at all. Under the correlation route the first component falls from 52.19% to 44.83%, and the seventh attribute takes over the second component outright, with a loading of 0.528 — larger than any other attribute carries there. The number of components needed to reach ninety percent of the variance goes from four to five. The second most important direction in the analysis is now dominated by a window that does not contain the target.

If you remember nothing else

  • There are two routes and they give different answers. Decomposing the covariance matrix and decomposing the correlation matrix are different analyses of the same data.
  • The covariance route is decided by whichever attribute has the largest numbers. On this set the variances span a factor of seven hundred thousand and the first component is ninety-five percent one attribute.
  • Rescaling an input changes the covariance-route answer and not the correlation-route answer. That is the test worth running on any method before trusting what it returns.
  • Standardizing is a decision, not a cleanup. It declares every attribute equally important, which is a claim about the attribute set rather than a property of it.
  • With exactly two standardized attributes the first component is always at forty-five degrees. The correlation sets the elongation and nothing else. Three or more attributes remove the degeneracy.
  • An equal share goes to attributes that earned nothing. A correctly computed attribute on the wrong interval took over the second component here.

Method

What is computed, and what is left out

Native units in this module, deliberately. Modules 01 and 02 multiply the amplitude-valued attributes by 1000 so that their readouts are round numbers. That factor is harmless there, because nothing in either module depends on the relative size of two attributes in unlike units. Here it would not be harmless: applying it to two of the six attributes and not to the other four would change the covariance-route answer, which is precisely the effect this module is about. So every number on this page is in the attribute's own units, and the display gain used elsewhere in the set is itself an example of the choice being examined. Run with a gain of 1000 on RMS amplitude and peak envelope, the covariance route returns a first component of 0.776 peak envelope and 0.507 RMS, a third distinct answer from the same rocks.

The two routes. The covariance route removes the mean of each attribute and decomposes the resulting covariance matrix. The correlation route removes the mean and divides by the standard deviation of each attribute, which makes the covariance matrix of the result the correlation matrix of the original. Both are computed by assets/multivar.js with the same symmetric eigensolver, and the only difference between them is the division.

The survey and the attributes are the same as modules 01 and 02: 72 by 72 traces, two channel systems, a levee wedge, a 30 Hz Ricker wavelet, footprint on a six-trace period and 12% noise. The six attributes are computed on samples 22 to 50. The seventh, RMS amplitude above the target, is computed on samples 2 to 16, which sits above the channel system entirely.

Step 4's left panel draws both attributes on one common numeric scale, in their own units, so that the angle on screen is the angle the covariance route works with. For a pair whose typical sizes differ by a factor of ten the cloud is a near-vertical sliver, which is an accurate picture of why one of the two is ignored. The right panel is the same traces standardized, on axes of four standard deviations. Neither panel is a distortion of the other; they are the two coordinate systems the two routes operate in.

Sign conventions. An eigenvector's sign is arbitrary, so multivar.js fixes it by making the largest-magnitude loading positive. Two runs that differ only in sign would otherwise produce maps with the color bar reversed for no reason a reader could see. Comparisons of loadings between the two routes in this module are unaffected, but a sign flip between two published figures of the same component means nothing.

Simplifications, stated plainly. Nothing here decimates before computing the matrix; every one of the 5184 traces is used, where a production run on a full volume would work from a subset. No attribute here is clipped or percentile-limited before the analysis, which some workflows do and which changes the variances and therefore the covariance-route answer. The standardization used is the ordinary one, subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation; robust alternatives using the median and a percentile range behave differently on attributes with long tails and are not shown.

Where this departs from production software. Packages differ in which route they take by default and in whether they say so. Some standardize silently, some offer it as an option whose label does not make clear which matrix is being decomposed, and some decompose the covariance matrix of data that a previous step already normalized. The test in step 2 settles the question for any of them from the outside: rescale one input by a large factor, run it again, and see whether the answer moved.
