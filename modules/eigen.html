Module 04  ·  Dimension reduction

Eigenvectors, loadings and scores

Module 01 found the widest direction by dragging a line through a cloud and writing down numbers. That works for two attributes and stops working almost immediately after. These five steps replace the search with arithmetic that returns the same answer, show where the second and third directions come from, and settle what the phrase "principal component" refers to, which is two different things depending on who is using it.

One component of the six-attribute set — its loadings

Choose a component — steps 3 to 5 follow it

Which component

All six attributes are standardized first, following module 03. Every eigenvalue on this page is therefore a share of six.

Sign

An eigenvector solves its equation just as well with every sign reversed. Step 5 is about what that does and does not change.

Work through the five steps in order, then try the exercises. Method and references are there when you want them.

1An eigenvector is an axis you can spin the thing about

The teapot from module 00, again

Start here if eigenvectors have never made sense. Forget the word for a moment. A teapot has three natural axes: a long one, a middling one and a short one. Those three are its eigenvectors. Nothing more mysterious than that.

Pick an axis and spin it. Watch how much of the pot you can see as it turns.

Why those three axes and not any other three

Spin a teapot about a randomly chosen axis and it wobbles: the thing lurches about because the axis does not line up with the shape. Spin it about its own long axis and it turns evenly. The three axes it turns evenly about are the three the shape itself picks out, and they are always at right angles to each other.

Those three axes are what comes out of the arithmetic. The eigenvalue that goes with each one is simply how far the pot extends along it — 0.483 along the long axis, 0.207 along the middling one, 0.152 along the short one, in the units this page uses.

The word eigenvector means a direction a matrix leaves pointing the same way, stretching it without turning it. For a rotation that is the axis the rotation turns about. For a covariance matrix, which is what is used here, it is an axis of the cloud. Both are the same idea: the direction the operation does not swing around.

Spinning about each of the three axes

How much you can see, all the way round

Spin it about

The axis you spin about is the one that stays in the picture. Spin about the long axis and it is always there, so the view never gets bad: it stays between 0.635 and 0.690. Spin about the short axis and the long one keeps swinging out of sight, so the view never gets good: 0.360 to 0.635. Spinning about the middle axis gives you the whole range, best and worst both.

2The same three axes, for something you cannot picture

Every direction in space, colored by its spread

Change the third attribute. Three bright spots appear. Those are the three eigenvectors, and finding them by sweeping would have meant trying 32,400 directions.

The longer version

With two attributes a direction is one angle. With three it takes two, and the sweep becomes the map below: every direction in three-dimensional attribute space, colored by the spread of the projection along it. The horizontal axis is the direction's bearing within the plane of the first two attributes and the vertical axis is how far it tilts toward the third.

Three maxima appear, marked with circles, and three minima between them. They are the three eigenvectors, and they are at right angles to each other in the space even though the map flattens that out. The eigenvalue of each is the value of the map at its marker.

The arithmetic that returns all three does not care how many attributes there are. The sweep does: two attributes need a hundred and eighty directions tried, three need about thirty thousand at the same spacing, and the six attributes in the header would need five angles and something like ten billion. That is the whole practical reason the eigenvector route is the one that gets used.

3The second component is the first component of what is left

Remove components and run it again

Remove one component, then two, then three. Watch the whole list shift one place left each time.

The longer version

Subtract from every attribute the part of it that the first component accounts for. What remains is a residual dataset with one less direction of spread in it. Run the same calculation on that residual and its first component is the original second component, exactly.

The bars below show the eigenvalues of the original six-attribute set and of the residual after removing however many components the slider is set to. Each removal shifts the whole list one place to the left and leaves a zero at the end. That is what ordering by variance means: the components are not chosen together, they are chosen one at a time, each from whatever the previous ones did not take.

Eigenvalues before and after removing components

Same component axis in both
Because each component is taken from the residual of the last, the directions come out at right angles to each other whether or not anything in the data is at right angles. That orthogonality is a property of the procedure. Module 06 is about what it costs when two geological effects are not independent enough to sit on perpendicular axes.

4Six numbers and five thousand numbers

Both are called the principal component

Six numbers on the left, 5184 on the right. Both of them get called the principal component.

The longer version

A component has two parts and they are different shapes. The loadings are one number per attribute, six in this set, saying how much of each attribute went into the direction. The scores are one number per trace, 5184 here, saying where each trace sits along it. The loadings define the component and the scores are what gets written out as a volume.

Both are routinely called the principal component. Statistics distinguishes them as loadings and scores; the AASPI documentation for its waveform programs calls the eigenvectors the initial training waveforms and the projections the principal components. Neither usage is wrong and both are common, so the question to ask of any figure labeled PC2 is which of the two it is showing. Six numbers and a map of a survey are not easily confused once the distinction is in mind.

The same component, both ways

Channel axes marked on the map

Which component

Reading the loadings is the only way to know what a component contains. A component whose largest loadings are on coherence and GLCM contrast is a texture image whatever number it was given, and predicting what its map will look like from the loadings alone, before looking, is a habit worth forming.

5The sign means nothing

Flip it in the header and watch the numbers

Flip the sign in the header. The map turns inside out. Not one measurable quantity moves.

The longer version

If a direction satisfies the eigenvector equation, so does the same direction reversed. Nothing in the arithmetic prefers one over the other, so the sign that comes back is whatever the implementation happened to settle on. Flipping it negates every loading and every score, which turns the map inside out, and leaves the eigenvalue, the share of variance and the shape of everything untouched.

The consequence for interpretation is small and easy to trip over. Two figures of the same component computed by two packages can be exact negatives of each other, and a reader comparing them without noticing will describe the channel as bright in one and dim in the other. The readouts below are the quantities that do not move.

The same component, both signs

Two maps, one color bar convention

Which component

The convention used throughout this module set is to make the largest-magnitude loading positive, which is one of several in use. Others fix the sign by the first loading, or by requiring a positive correlation with a chosen attribute, or leave it to whatever the solver produced. Module 08 meets a harder version of the same problem, where an ICA component's order is undetermined as well as its sign.

Why it matters

Where this shows up in real work

This module contains the only piece of linear algebra the set needs. Everything after it is either a decision about what to do with the components or a different rule for choosing directions.

  • Reading a QC display. Packages plot eigenvectors for quality control, often against an axis of attribute number or window sample. Knowing that those are the loadings and not the output volumes is the difference between reading the plot and guessing at it.
  • Describing a result. "PC2 shows the channel" is ambiguous until it is clear whether the speaker means the map or the weights. The weights cannot show a channel; they are six numbers.
  • Comparing two runs. A sign flip between packages is meaningless and looks alarming. A change in the loadings is meaningful and looks like nothing at all on a map.
  • Knowing why the components are orthogonal. They are orthogonal because each was taken from the residual of the last, not because the geology is. That distinction is the whole of module 06.

Two points that are commonly confused. The first is that a large eigenvalue makes a component important. The eigenvalue is the variance of the score and nothing else; module 01 already showed a component holding a sixth of the variance carrying the better image. The second is that eigenvectors are somehow discovered in the data. The covariance matrix has them the moment it is written down, and what the data decided was the matrix.

The eigensolver in this set is a cyclic Jacobi rotation, chosen because it is short enough to read and cannot return a complex answer for a real symmetric input. Production libraries use faster methods that return the same numbers.

Exercises

  1. Check the shortcut against the search. Why: an eigenvector is worth accepting as a labor-saving device before it is accepted as a definition. In step 1, choose RMS amplitude and coherence, read the angle at the top of the swept curve and the angle the eigenvector gives, then read the eigenvalue and the height of the curve at that angle.
    HintBoth give 135.00 degrees, and the eigenvalue is 1.6487, which is the height of the curve at its maximum. The second eigenvalue is 0.3513, which is the height at the minimum, and the two add to 2 — the number of attributes, because both were standardized. The residual on the eigenvector equation is around 10 to the minus sixteen, which is the arithmetic working exactly rather than approximately. The angle is 135 rather than 45 because the correlation is negative; module 03 covers why two standardized attributes only ever give those two answers.
  2. Count the directions a sweep would have to try. Why: sweeping becomes impossible very quickly, and the numbers show how quickly. In step 2, note that a direction in three dimensions needs two angles. Work out how many directions a sweep at one-degree spacing would try for three attributes, and then for the six in the header.
    HintTwo attributes need one angle: 180 directions. Three need two angles: 180 by 180, about thirty-two thousand, which is the map in step 2. Six attributes need five angles, which at the same spacing is 180 to the fifth power, about a hundred and ninety billion directions. Solving the eigenvector problem for six attributes is a six-by-six matrix and takes a few thousand arithmetic operations. Twelve attributes would make the sweep hopeless and leave the matrix problem still small.
  3. Remove the first component and run it again. Why: this is where the ordering comes from, and it explains why the components are perpendicular. In step 3, set the slider to 1 and compare the eigenvalues of the residual against those of the original set. Then set it to 2 and 3.
    HintThe original eigenvalues are 3.1569, 1.3018, 0.7227, 0.4192, 0.2675, 0.1320. After removing one component the residual gives 1.3018, 0.7227, 0.4192, 0.2675, 0.1320 and then zero — the same list shifted one place, with the first component's variance gone and nothing else touched. Removing two shifts it twice. The first component of the residual is the second component of the original to within a hundredth of a degree, which is the sense in which each component is the widest direction in whatever the previous ones left behind.
  4. Predict a map from six numbers. Why: the loadings are the component, and reading them is a faster way to know what a volume contains than looking at it. For each of components 1, 2 and 3, read the loadings in the header, decide what the map should look like, then look at step 4.
    HintComponent 1 loads 0.506 on RMS amplitude, 0.382 on peak envelope, 0.380 on GLCM contrast, 0.260 on peak frequency, −0.434 on spectral bandwidth and −0.444 on coherence. Everything that makes a trace bright and locally variable pushes one way and everything that makes it smooth and broadband pushes the other, so the map is essentially a brightness-and-disruption image and both channels appear in it. Component 2 loads 0.582 on peak frequency and 0.521 on peak envelope against −0.524 on GLCM contrast, with almost nothing on RMS amplitude at −0.044 — a tuning image rather than a brightness one. Component 3 is dominated by peak frequency at 0.633 with bandwidth at 0.499 opposing coherence at −0.397. One caution on reading loadings this way: they say what a component is made of arithmetically, not what it responds to in the ground. A component loading heavily on amplitude could be tracking impedance contrast, bed thickness near tuning, or fluid, and the loadings are identical in all three cases. The eigenvalues are 3.1569, 1.3018 and 0.7227, holding 52.62%, 21.70% and 12.04% of the variance.
  5. Flip a sign and find what changed. Why: this is a difference between two published figures that means nothing, and it is easy to spend time on. In step 5, flip the sign of component 2 and compare every readout before and after.
    HintEvery loading changes sign and every score changes sign, so the map inverts: what was red is blue. The eigenvalue stays at 1.3018, the share of variance stays at 21.70%, the variance of the score stays at 1.3018, and the correlation of the score with any other component's score stays at zero. Nothing measurable moved. The practical rule is that two figures of the same component from different software should be compared on the shape of their patterns and the relative magnitudes of their loadings, never on which way up they are.

If you remember nothing else

  • An eigenvector of the covariance matrix is the direction the sweep was looking for. Same answer, one step instead of a search that grows with every attribute added.
  • The eigenvalue is the variance of the score along that direction. Not a score, not a weight: the spread of the data along the component, in whatever units the analysis used.
  • Each component is taken from the residual of the last. That is where the ordering comes from and why the components are perpendicular to each other.
  • Loadings and scores are different objects. One number per attribute against one number per trace. Both get called the principal component, so ask which is on screen.
  • The scores of two components are uncorrelated, and with the means removed they are orthogonal as well. Measured here at ten to the minus fifteen.
  • The sign is arbitrary. Flipping it negates the loadings and the map and changes no measurable quantity at all.

Method

What is computed, and what is left out

The eigensolver is a cyclic Jacobi rotation in assets/multivar.js. It repeatedly zeroes one off-diagonal element of the matrix at a time until everything off the diagonal is negligible; what remains on the diagonal are the eigenvalues and the accumulated rotations are the eigenvectors. It is slower than the methods production libraries use and it is short enough to read, numerically sound on matrices this size, and incapable of returning a complex answer for a real symmetric input. It is checked in verify-multivar.js against an identity matrix, a rotated ellipse of known angle and eigenvalues, a perfectly redundant pair whose second eigenvalue must vanish, and a four-by-four whose trace must be preserved.

The standardization. All six attributes are Z-scored before the matrix is formed, which is the correlation route of module 03. That is why the six eigenvalues sum to exactly 6.000000: standardizing gives each attribute a variance of one, and the eigenvalues redistribute that total without creating or destroying any.

Step 1's two curves. The swept curve evaluates the variance of the projection at every whole degree, computed as uᵀCu rather than by projecting all 5184 traces at each angle, which gives the same number for a great deal less work. The marker is the eigenvector from the solver. The residual in the readout is the largest element of Cv − λv.

Step 2's map covers every direction in the three-dimensional space by two angles: a bearing in the plane of the first two attributes, and a tilt toward the third. Every direction appears twice on it, once as itself and once as its opposite, which is why the maxima come in pairs across the map. Only the upper half of the tilt range is drawn, since the lower half repeats it. The color scale is fixed to the range zero to three so that changing the third attribute changes the picture rather than the scale.

Step 3's residual is formed by reconstructing the data from the first k components and subtracting that from the input, then dividing by the original standard deviations so that the residual is compared on the same footing as the input rather than re-standardized to its own spread. Re-standardizing the residual would rescale each attribute by whatever survived, which changes the answer and is not what deflation means.

Simplifications, stated plainly. Nothing here treats the case of repeated eigenvalues, where the directions within the repeated subspace are genuinely undetermined rather than merely sign-ambiguous; it does not arise on real attribute data but it is the reason a textbook statement of uniqueness carries a condition. The Jacobi solver is run to a fixed tolerance rather than adaptively. No decimation is used, where a production run on a full volume would compute the matrix from a subset of traces and apply the result to all of them.

Where this departs from production software. In the solver only, and not in the answers. Implementations differ in the sign convention they apply, in whether they return eigenvalues normalized to sum to one, and in whether they scale the scores to unit variance before writing them out. The last of those matters: a score volume that has been rescaled no longer has the eigenvalue as its variance, and the ranking then lives only in the eigenvalue listing.
