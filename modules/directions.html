Module 01  ·  Dimension reduction

A direction through a cloud

Two attributes computed over the same survey can be plotted against each other, one point per trace. The cloud that results has a shape, and that shape is the only thing every method in this set works from. These five steps push a line through the cloud by hand and measure what happens, which is the whole of principal component analysis before any matrix is written down.

Two attributes, one point per trace — drag the direction through it

Choose the pair, then choose a direction — every panel updates as you drag

Which two attributes

All three are amplitude measurements on the same window, so both axes can be drawn at the same number of amplitude units per pixel and the angle on screen is the angle in the data.

The direction

Work through the five steps in order, then try the exercises. Method and references are there when you want them.

1Two maps, replotted as one cloud

The same numbers, arranged two ways

Why this step is here. An attribute map is arranged by where each value came from. A crossplot throws that away and arranges the same values by what they are. Almost everything that follows happens in the second arrangement, so it is worth being certain the two hold identical information.

Two maps below. The crossplot above the tabs is those same numbers, replotted: one point per trace, its value in the left map across and its value in the right map up.

Notice the cloud leans. That lean is the whole subject of this module set.

Why it leans

The two attributes are measuring overlapping things, so a trace with a strong reflection tends to be strong in both. That overlap is what makes one of them partly predictable from the other, and it is the reason a set of twelve attributes is rarely twelve independent pieces of information.

The two attribute maps

Channel axes marked in white on both
Turning the noise up stretches the cloud, and not evenly. The noise enters both attributes from the same samples on the same trace, so what they inherit is shared rather than independent, and the two inherit it at different rates. Watch which of the two spreads in the readout moves and which one stays where it is.

2Pushing one direction through it

Drag the angle and watch the histogram

Drag the angle slider. Every trace drops onto the line; how far along it lands is its one number. The histogram below counts where they land.

The cloud never moves. The histogram does.

The longer version

Dropping a point onto a line at right angles is called a projection, and the number it gives replaces the two the trace started with. The width of the histogram is the spread of that projection, measured as variance. Turning the line asks a different question of the same points, which is why one panel changes and the other does not.

Where the traces land along the line

Fixed axis, so the width is comparable between angles
The horizontal axis of this histogram is held fixed whatever the angle. An axis that resized itself to the data would make every projection look the same width, which is the one thing this step is trying to show is untrue.

3Finding the widest direction by hand

Sweep the angle across the whole range

One angle gives one number. Every angle gives the curve below. Its peak is the first principal component — and you just found it by dragging.

The longer version

The curve runs from zero to a hundred and eighty degrees and then repeats, because a direction and its opposite give the same line and therefore the same spread. It has exactly one maximum and one minimum and they are ninety degrees apart.

Nothing here could not have been found by moving the slider and writing numbers down, which is the point: the eigenvector arithmetic in module 04 is a shortcut to this answer, not a different one.

Spread as a function of direction

Your angle in crimson, the widest in teal
The curve is a cosine riding on a constant, which is why it is smooth and has exactly one peak. That shape is a property of variance, not of these particular attributes, and it is the reason the widest direction is always unique unless the cloud is perfectly round.

4What is left in the other direction

Both histograms share one axis

Two histograms: along your direction, and across it. Move the angle and watch them trade. Whatever one gains the other loses, exactly.

The total never changes. Only how unevenly it is split.

The longer version

The two variances sum to the same number at every angle, so the question is never how much spread there is, only how unevenly it is divided. At the widest direction the division is as uneven as it can be, and that is the sense in which one number then stands in for two.

Along the direction, and across it

One shared axis, both fixed

5Putting the one number back on the map

Two attributes in, one map out

Every trace has one number now, and a position in the survey. So draw it as a map. Two volumes in, one volume out.

Move the angle and watch the channels move between the two output maps. The white lines mark where they are.

The longer version

Four maps are drawn: the two input attributes, the projection along the chosen direction, and the projection across it. At some angles the channels are clearer in the along map, at others they survive only in the across map, and at the widest direction the across map is mostly what the two attributes disagreed about.

This is what a dimension reduction actually delivers. The direction chosen is what decides what the surviving volume contains.

Inputs and projections, same survey

Channel axes marked on all four
Each map is scaled to its own range so that its structure is visible. The across map is much weaker than the along map at the widest direction, and displaying it at its own scale is the only way to see what is in it — at the shared scale it would be nearly blank. Module 05 returns to this, because a component displayed at its own scale can look far more important than its eigenvalue says it is.

Why it matters

Where this shows up in real work

No method is named in this module and no matrix appears in it. What it establishes is the picture that every later module manipulates, and the vocabulary that machine learning papers assume their readers already have.

  • Reading a crossplot. Interpreters make two-attribute crossplots routinely and read clusters off them. The tilt of the cloud, which is usually ignored, is the quantity every method in this set is built on.
  • Knowing what a component is. A component is a weighted sum of the input attributes. The weights are the direction. A component volume is therefore an ordinary attribute arithmetic operation, and can be reproduced in any package that can add two volumes with coefficients.
  • Judging whether an attribute is worth adding. If a new attribute lands the cloud in a thin sliver, it is carrying little the existing attributes did not. The correlation matrix in module 02 is the fast version of that check.
  • Following the language. Feature, sample, dimension, projection and variance are used throughout the literature without definition. Every one of them is defined here, and clicking a term in the text opens its definition.

Two points that are commonly confused. The first is that the widest direction is the most useful one. It is the widest, which is a statement about spread and not about geology; module 06 is where that distinction is made carefully, and step 5 already shows angles at which the narrow direction carries the clearer channel. The second is that projecting onto a direction discards the other attribute. It does not: it forms a weighted sum of both, and what is discarded is only whatever was left over in the perpendicular direction.

Everything in this module was done with two attributes because two can be drawn. The arithmetic is identical for twelve, and the only thing lost is the picture.

Exercises

  1. Find the widest direction without using step 3. Why: the answer that eigenvectors deliver is an answer you can also reach by hand, and having reached it once makes the arithmetic in module 04 a shortcut rather than a rule. With RMS & envelope selected, use step 2 alone. Move the angle in tens from 0 to 170 and write down the variance readout each time, then narrow in on the largest. Before starting: the two attributes have a correlation of 0.65. Will the widest direction be at 45 degrees, above it, or below it? Commit to an answer and a reason.
    HintThe widest direction is at 59 degrees, not 45. Variance reads 104.8 at 0 degrees, rises through 213.0 at 30 and 251.3 at 45, peaks at 263.9 at 59, and falls back to 206.5 at 90. The reason it is not at 45 is that the two attributes have different spreads of their own: peak envelope has a standard deviation of 14.4 amplitude units against 10.2 for RMS, so the cloud is stretched more vertically than horizontally and the widest direction leans toward the vertical axis. Two things control the tilt, the correlation and the relative spread, and only the second of them is about the units the attributes happen to be reported in. Module 03 is about what to do with that.
  2. Compare the three pairs. Why: redundancy between two attributes is visible as a shape before it is computed as a number. Step through the three attribute pairs and record, for each, the correlation, the widest angle, and the share of the total spread that the widest direction holds.
    HintRMS & mean absolute amplitude: correlation 0.96, widest at 46 degrees, holding 98.1% of the spread. RMS & envelope: correlation 0.65, widest at 59 degrees, 84.8%. Envelope & mean absolute amplitude: correlation 0.47, widest at 28 degrees, 77.0%. The first pair is nearly a straight line, which says that mean absolute amplitude adds almost nothing to RMS amplitude — the two are different summaries of the same window and they rise and fall together. A pair that plots as a line is a pair where one of the two could be dropped.
  3. Check that the total spread does not change. Why: this is the conservation statement underneath every scree plot, and it is what makes "percent of variance explained" a meaningful quantity. In step 4, read the along and across variances at 0, 30, 59 and 120 degrees, and add each pair.
    HintFor RMS & envelope: 104.8 + 206.5, 213.0 + 98.2, 263.9 + 47.3, and 98.2 + 213.0. Every pair sums to 311.3. The total spread belongs to the cloud and not to the direction chosen, so rotating the line redistributes variance without creating or destroying any. That is why the eigenvalues of a covariance matrix always sum to the total variance, and why a component can be quoted as a percentage of it.
  4. Turn the noise up and watch which of the two attributes moves. Why: noise reaches an attribute set through the seismic rather than through the maps, and two attributes computed on the same window do not inherit it at the same rate. With RMS & envelope, set the noise to 0%, then 24%, then 40%. Read the two spreads in step 1 each time, then the correlation, the widest angle and the share. Before starting: noise has no preferred direction in the survey. Commit to whether that means it will leave the tilt of the cloud alone.
    HintIt does not leave the tilt alone, and it swings it by fifteen degrees. The spread of RMS amplitude hardly moves across the whole range, from 10.23 to 10.37 amplitude units, because RMS averages over twenty-eight samples and independent noise largely cancels in that average. The spread of peak envelope nearly doubles, from 12.97 to 20.52, because it is the largest value in the window and the largest value in a noisy window grows with the noise. The widest direction leans toward the attribute whose spread is growing, swinging from 55 degrees at no noise to 70 degrees at 40%. Two further things are worth reading off. The correlation barely changes, 0.663 to 0.624, so a correlation matrix would not have flagged any of this. And the share held by the widest direction rises, from 84.3% to 88.9% — the noisier data looked more compressible. Both attributes drew their noise from the same samples, so what they gained was shared, and shared variation is exactly what a first component collects.
  5. Find an angle where the narrow direction is the more useful map. Why: the ranking that dimension reduction produces is a ranking by spread, and the most geologically informative image is not obliged to be at the top of it. In step 5, set the angle to 59 degrees and compare the along map with the across map. Then look at the across map along the younger channel axis in particular.
    HintAt 59 degrees the along map holds 84.8% of the spread and is largely a brightness map: both channels appear, and so does the acquisition footprint, because whatever makes a trace strong makes it strong in both attributes. The across map holds 15.2% and carries what the two attributes disagreed about. Measured as the separation between the younger channel axis and its own margins, in pooled standard deviations, the across map reaches 5.39 against 0.28 for the along map — and against 2.79 for RMS amplitude on its own and 1.16 for peak envelope on its own. The narrow direction is not merely better than the wide one here, it is about twice as good as either input attribute was. The footprint goes the other way: measured as the part of each map that follows the six-trace acquisition period, the along map carries 0.25 of a standard deviation and the across map 0.11. So the component holding a sixth of the variance carries the better image and less of the artifact, and nothing in the eigenvalues would have said so.

If you remember nothing else

  • A dataset is a cloud in a space whose axes are the features. Two attributes give a plane that can be drawn. Twelve give a space that cannot, and behaves the same way.
  • A projection is a weighted sum. Choosing a direction and projecting onto it is identical to adding the input volumes with coefficients. A component volume is that sum.
  • Rotating the direction redistributes the spread and never changes the total. Along and across always add to the same number, which is what makes a percentage of variance meaningful.
  • The widest direction is set by two things. How strongly the attributes are correlated, and how large their individual spreads are. The second depends on the units they are reported in, which is the problem module 03 takes up.
  • A tilted cloud means redundancy. The thinner the cloud, the more nearly one attribute is a restatement of the other, and the less the second one is adding.
  • Widest is not most useful. The ranking is by spread. Whether a direction corresponds to anything in the ground is a separate question that this arithmetic cannot answer, and step 5 shows an angle where the narrow direction carries the clearer image.

Method

What is computed, and what is left out

The survey. A synthetic slab of 72 by 72 traces at 2 ms sampling is built in the browser by assets/attrfield.js from an impedance model: background shale with a few interbeds whose depths drift across the survey, a younger sinuous channel filled with sand, an older and thinner channel at a different orientation, and a levee wedge either side of the younger one. Reflection coefficients are convolved with a 30 Hz Ricker wavelet. Acquisition footprint is applied as a multiplicative striping on a six-trace period in both directions, and random noise is added at the level set by the slider. Nothing is a stored image.

The attributes. All three are computed over the same window, samples 22 to 50. RMS amplitude is the root mean square of the window. Mean absolute amplitude is the average of the absolute values. Peak envelope is the largest value in the window of the envelope, computed as the magnitude of the analytic trace by the Fourier method, zeroing the negative frequencies and doubling the positive ones. All three are multiplied by 1000 for display, so that readouts are in round numbers; the factor is arbitrary and cancels out of every correlation and every angle.

The crossplot axes. Both attributes have their means removed before plotting, so the origin is the center of the cloud and the direction line passes through it. Both axes span the same number of amplitude units and are drawn into a square box, which means the angle read off the screen is the angle in the data. This is the reason the three attribute pairs offered are all amplitude measurements: a pair in unlike units could not be drawn this way, and the angle would be an artifact of the axis choice rather than a property of the data. That problem is the subject of module 03.

The variance curve in step 3 is computed by projecting all 5184 traces at every degree from 0 to 179 and taking the variance of each projection. It is not fitted or smoothed. Variance is computed dividing by the number of samples rather than by one less than it, which makes no visible difference at this sample count and is stated so that a reader checking the numbers against their own calculation knows which convention is on screen.

Simplifications, stated plainly. The survey is 2D in the sense that each trace contributes one point; no attribute here uses dip, and the coherence and curvature attributes that would need it are held back to later modules. The mixing of channel and background is geometric rather than physical: there is no compaction, no lateral velocity variation and no multiple energy. The noise is Gaussian and independent from trace to trace, which real noise is not. The footprint is perfectly periodic, which real footprint is not. Each of those simplifications makes the effect under discussion easier to see and none of them changes the arithmetic being taught.

The separation numbers quoted in the exercises compare the traces within half a channel width of the younger channel axis against those between 1.1 and 2.0 channel widths from it, as the difference of the two means divided by the pooled standard deviation. It is a convenient summary for comparing one map against another and it is not a standard seismic measurement. The footprint numbers are the projection of each map onto the six-trace striping that was put into the survey, expressed as a fraction of the map's own standard deviation.

Where this departs from production software. Nowhere yet. Nothing in this module is an algorithm, only projection and variance, which every package computes identically. The departures begin in module 05, where the choice of how many components to keep is made by a convention that differs between implementations, and each module from there carries its own list.
