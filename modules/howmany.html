Module 05  ·  Dimension reduction

How many components to keep

Six attributes give six components and nobody carries six. Choosing where to stop is presented in most write-ups as a matter of reading a graph, and on this attribute set five standard ways of reading that graph return five different numbers. These steps run all of them, then look at what each choice actually discards, as a picture rather than as a percentage.

2Five rules, five answers

Press each button in the header

Press all five rule buttons. They return one, two, three, four and five components.

The longer version

Several rules for where to cut are in common use, and all of them are defensible. The broken-stick rule compares each eigenvalue against what it would have been if the total variance had been divided up at random, and keeps the components that beat that. The Kaiser rule keeps components whose eigenvalue exceeds one, on the argument that a standardized attribute carries a variance of one and a component carrying less than that has done worse than an attribute would have. The retained-variance rules keep enough components to reach a stated fraction of the total.

On these six attributes they return one, two, three, four and five. Every number between the extremes is somebody's correct answer, and the choice between them is not settled by anything in the data.

What each rule keeps

Eigenvalues against the thresholds each rule applies

Apply a rule

The rules are not competing estimates of one true number. They answer different questions: whether a component beats chance, whether it beats an attribute, and how much of the total is wanted. Which question applies depends on what the components are going to be used for, which is the subject of step 5.

The longer version

A percentage of variance discarded is a summary of the residual and not a description of it. The residual is a set of maps, one per attribute, and it can be looked at directly. Random noise thrown away looks like random noise. Geology thrown away looks like geology.

The panels below are the residual of every attribute at the current cut, each drawn at its own scale so that whatever is in it is visible. At a cut of one component the channels are plainly still there in most of them. By four they are mostly gone from the residual and what remains is closer to noise and footprint. That transition, looked at rather than computed, is a better basis for the decision than any of the rules in step 2.

The longer version

A retained-variance figure is a total across all the attributes at once. It says nothing about how the shortfall is distributed between them, and it is never distributed evenly. The bars below give, for each attribute, how much of its own spread the reconstruction fails to account for at the current cut.

At that cut peak frequency is reconstructed to within eleven percent of its own spread and spectral bandwidth to within eight, while coherence is still missing thirty-five percent and GLCM contrast thirty-seven. An attribute can be almost entirely absent from a reconstruction that a headline number describes as ninety-three percent complete.

5Which component has the channel in it

Compare the two panels against each other

Compare the two rows of bars. The component holding half the variance is the worst of the six at finding the channel.

The longer version

Everything so far has ranked the components by variance, because that is the only thing the method knows about them. The panels below add a second ranking: how well each component separates the axis of the younger channel from its own margins, measured in pooled standard deviations. The two rankings are not the same ranking.

The first component holds 52.62% of the variance and separates the channel at 0.22, which is no separation at all. The second holds 21.70% and separates at 3.11; the third holds 12.04% and separates at 2.41. The work is being done by the two components below the top, and a rule that kept only the first would have discarded both of them.

The lower panel is the same measurement on the six input attributes, and it makes a point that is easy to leave out of a lecture on dimension reduction. Peak frequency on its own separates the channel at 6.40, twice as well as the best component. For this particular target on this particular survey, the dimension reduction did not improve on the best single input.

Variance held against channel separation

Components above, input attributes below

What to measure

The separation statistic uses the model's own knowledge of where the channel is, which no real survey provides. It stands in for an interpreter's judgment of whether a map is useful, and it is used here because a number can be plotted and a judgment cannot.

Why it matters

Where this shows up in real work

The number of components kept is a parameter every implementation asks for, and the guidance that comes with it is usually a single convention stated without alternatives. On a real attribute set the conventions disagree by a factor of several.

  • RGB blending fixes the number at three. Not because three is right, but because a display has three channels. The question then becomes which three, and the answer is not obliged to be the top three.
  • Noise reduction wants the break. Reconstructing from the components above a break and discarding the rest is a filter, and it works when the break exists and the discarded components are noise. Step 3 is how to check the second half of that.
  • Feeding a classifier. Unsupervised classification on components is common, and the count chosen upstream sets what the classifier can possibly see. A feature that lives in a discarded component cannot be recovered downstream.
  • Reading a reported figure. "Four components, ninety-three percent of the variance" is a statement about a total. Whether the attribute that mattered is among the well-reconstructed ones is a separate question and usually goes unasked.

Two points that are commonly confused. The first is that variance explained measures how much of the useful content was kept. It measures how much of the spread was kept, and spread includes noise and footprint, which the reconstruction preserves as faithfully as it preserves geology. The second is that dimension reduction necessarily improves on the inputs. It reorganizes them. Whether the reorganization helps depends on the target, and step 5 shows a target for which one of the original attributes was better than anything the method produced.

None of this argues against running the method. It argues for looking at the residual before accepting the cut, which takes about a minute and is skipped almost universally.

Exercises

  1. Run all five rules on the same six attributes. Why: the disagreement is the point, and it is larger than most write-ups suggest. Press each of the five rule buttons in turn and record what each returns and what fraction of the variance it keeps. Before starting: how far apart do you expect the five answers to be? Commit to a range.
    HintBroken stick keeps 1 and 52.62% of the variance. Kaiser keeps 2 and 74.31%. The 80% rule keeps 3 and 86.36%. The 90% rule keeps 4 and 93.34%. The 95% rule keeps 5 and 97.80%. Five rules, five answers, one through five out of six possible. The eigenvalues they are reading are 3.16, 1.30, 0.72, 0.42, 0.27 and 0.13, and nothing in that list is a cliff. The honest summary is that the scree plot did not answer the question, and the rules are conventions applied in its place.
  2. Look at the residual instead of the number. Why: the question a rule cannot answer is whether what was discarded was noise, and that one can simply be looked at. In step 3, walk the cut from 1 to 5 and watch the six residual maps, looking specifically for the channel axes marked on them.
    HintAt a cut of 1 the channels are visible in the residual of nearly every attribute, which means a great deal of geology was discarded — and the broken-stick rule would have stopped there. By 3 they have faded from most of the panels. By 4 the residuals are dominated by the six-trace footprint striping and by speckle, which is what a reader wants to see before accepting a cut. Be careful with the word noise here: a residual that looks like speckle could be random noise, or it could be real geology finer than the attributes can resolve. What the panel shows is that the discarded components no longer contain anything organized at the scale of the channels, which is a weaker statement than saying they contain nothing. Note that the residual panels each use their own scale, so a nearly empty residual and a substantial one look equally emphatic; the sizes are in step 4.
  3. Find the attribute that ninety percent left behind. Why: a retained-variance figure is a total, and totals hide their own distribution. Set the cut to 4, which the 90% rule selects, and read the per-attribute residual bars in step 4.
    HintAt four components the total retained is 93.34%, and the residual as a fraction of each attribute's own spread reads 0.291 for RMS amplitude, 0.201 for peak envelope, 0.112 for peak frequency, 0.084 for spectral bandwidth, 0.346 for coherence and 0.368 for GLCM contrast. The two texture-related attributes are each still missing more than a third of their spread inside a reconstruction described as ninety-three percent complete. Going to five components collapses GLCM contrast to 0.018 and coherence to 0.150, because the fifth component is largely those two. A cut chosen on the total would have thrown away almost the whole of the attribute pair a texture interpretation depends on.
  4. Rank the components by variance, then by usefulness. Why: the method's ranking is by spread, and it is not obliged to agree with anything an interpreter cares about. In step 5, compare the fraction of variance each component holds against how well it separates the young channel axis from its margins.
    HintBy variance the order is 52.62%, 21.70%, 12.04%, 6.99%, 4.46%, 2.20%. By channel separation the order is 0.22, 3.11, 2.41, 0.65, 0.39, 0.89. The largest component is the worst of the six at the job, and the two that do the work are the second and third. A broken-stick cut at one component keeps only the component that separates the channel least well of any of them. The Kaiser cut at two keeps the best one and discards the second best.
  5. Compare the components against the raw attributes. Why: a method is worth running if it beats what went into it, and that is a testable claim rather than an assumption. Still in step 5, read the lower panel, which applies the same separation measurement to the six input attributes.
    HintPeak frequency separates the channel at 6.40 on its own. The best component manages 3.11. RMS amplitude at 3.38 is also competitive with the best component, and coherence at 0.15 is worse than anything. So for this target the dimension reduction produced nothing better than an attribute that was already on the list, and the honest use of the analysis here is as a way of finding out which attribute that was. Switch the measurement to acquisition footprint and a different picture appears. The artifact enters the set mainly through peak envelope, which carries 0.241 of it, and RMS amplitude at 0.122; peak frequency and GLCM contrast carry essentially none, at 0.001. Among the components it does not spread evenly: it concentrates in the fourth at 0.213, which holds 7.46% of the variance. So a cut at three components would have removed most of the footprint along with whatever else the fourth was carrying, and the 90% rule, which keeps four, would have kept it. Neither rule was considering the footprint at all.

If you remember nothing else

  • Real scree plots usually have no cliff. These eigenvalues fall away smoothly from 3.16 to 0.13 with nothing that can be called a break.
  • The standard rules disagree by a factor of five. Broken stick, Kaiser, 80%, 90% and 95% return one, two, three, four and five components on the same six attributes.
  • Look at the residual. If the channels are still visible in what was discarded, the cut was too early, and that takes a minute to check.
  • A retained-variance figure is a total. At 93.34% retained, two of these six attributes are still missing more than a third of their own spread.
  • The variance ranking is not a usefulness ranking. The component holding half the variance separates the channel worst of the six; the second and third do the work.
  • Check the method against its own inputs. Here one raw attribute separated the channel twice as well as any component, which is a result worth knowing rather than hiding.

Method

What is computed, and what is left out

The survey, the attributes and the standardization are the same as module 04: six attributes on samples 22 to 50 of a 72 by 72 trace synthetic slab, Z-scored before the matrix is formed, so the six eigenvalues sum to exactly six.

The five rules. The retained-variance rules keep the smallest number of components whose cumulative share reaches the stated fraction. The Kaiser rule keeps components with an eigenvalue above one, which is meaningful only on standardized data, where one is the variance of a single attribute; applied to a covariance matrix in native units the threshold means nothing. The broken-stick rule compares each eigenvalue against the expected length of the ith longest piece when a stick of length p is broken at p−1 random points, which for six attributes gives expected shares of 40.83%, 24.17%, 15.83%, 10.28%, 6.11% and 2.78%, and keeps components while they exceed it. It is the strictest of the five here and it stops at one.

The reconstruction rebuilds each attribute from the first k components by adding back each component multiplied by its loadings, then restores the original standard deviation and mean. Using all six returns the input to within 10⁻¹⁵. The residual is the input minus the reconstruction, and the residual figure quoted for each attribute is the standard deviation of its residual divided by the standard deviation of the attribute itself, so it is a fraction of that attribute's own spread rather than of the total.

The separation statistic in step 5 compares traces within half a channel width of the younger channel axis against traces between 1.1 and 2.0 channel widths from it, as the difference of the two means divided by the pooled standard deviation. It is the same statistic used in module 01 and it is a convenience for this teaching set rather than a standard seismic measurement. The footprint statistic is the projection of a map onto the six-trace striping that was put into the survey, as a fraction of the map's own standard deviation.

Simplifications, stated plainly. The separation statistic uses the model's own record of where the channel is, which no real survey supplies; on real data the equivalent judgment is made by an interpreter looking at a map. The residual panels are each scaled to their own range, which makes their contents visible and makes a small residual look as emphatic as a large one, so the sizes are given separately as bars. Nothing here decimates before computing the matrix. The analysis is on one stratal slab; a component count chosen on one interval does not transfer to another.

Where this departs from production software. In the choice offered rather than the arithmetic. Implementations typically expose one rule, most often a retained-variance threshold, and some apply it without reporting the eigenvalues at all. AASPI's ICA implementation uses a retained-variance criterion of ninety percent, treating the variance above that as signal and the remainder as noise; on the Taranaki dataset that returned four independent components at 94.04%. The number a rule returns is a property of the rule and of the attribute set, and moving either one moves the answer.
