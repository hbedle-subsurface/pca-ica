Looking at it from the right direction

Nothing in this module is about seismic data, for the first three steps. It is about a teapot, seen from several directions, because the whole of dimension reduction is already in that problem and a teapot is a thing you can recognize when you have found a good view of it. Attributes are not. By the end the same arithmetic will have been applied to both, and the difference between them will be the reason the rest of the set exists.

The same teapot, three ways

Spout in crimson, handle in teal
The teapot is not built lined up with the axes. It sits at an angle to them, on purpose. A set of seismic attributes is an arbitrary coordinate system in exactly the same way — nothing arranged for coherence and peak frequency to line up with a channel — so a teapot that conveniently pointed along an axis would be teaching the wrong lesson.

What the bar is actually measuring

Project every point of the teapot onto the flat picture and add up how spread out that picture is: the variance left to right plus the variance up and down. A view that squashes the pot gives a small number, a view that opens it out gives a large one. Nothing in that calculation knows what a teapot is — it is counting how far apart the points land.

3The best direction is part of all three axes

Press "The widest" in the header

Here is the view you were hunting for, written down:

across = 0.840 x + 0.533 y − 0.097 z
up     = 0.359 x − 0.412 y + 0.837 z

Neither one is x, or y, or z. Each is a bit of all three. That is the whole idea of this module set, and everything after here is that sentence with more dimensions in it.

The longer version

There is arithmetic that finds this without any searching, and module 04 builds it. It gives a view from about −61 degrees around and 33 degrees over, with a spread of 0.666 against 0.454, 0.573 and 0.618 for the front, side and top.

What matters is not that small improvement in the number. It is what the answer is made of. The coordinate system a thing arrives in — x, y and z for a teapot, coherence and amplitude and frequency for a survey — was not chosen to suit the thing being looked at, so the good view is a combination rather than one of them.

The two picture axes, as recipes

How much of each original axis went into each

Jump to a view

There were three directions available and the picture used two of them, so one was thrown away. For this teapot the two kept hold 81.0% of the total spread, and the discarded one holds the remaining 19.0%. Going from three numbers per point to two, and losing a fifth of the spread, is a dimension reduction. It is the same trade every module after this one is about.

4The same arithmetic, on something you cannot recognize

Three attributes instead of three axes

Same thing, but each point is now a seismic trace and the three axes are three attributes. Drag it. Nothing about the arithmetic changed.

The best view is a mixture again: 0.393 RMS + 0.735 envelope + 0.553 peak, holding 93.9% against 76.9%, 54.4% and 68.7% for the three axis views.

One thing is completely different, and it is the important one. You knew when the teapot looked right. Nobody knows what this is supposed to look like.

The longer version

The three attributes are RMS amplitude, peak envelope and peak absolute amplitude, computed over the same window on 5184 traces. Every trace has three numbers, exactly as every point on the teapot had three numbers, and the cloud they make is a cloud in three dimensions in exactly the same sense.

The arithmetic does not know or care which is which. What it cannot supply is the recognition — with the teapot you could tell a good view from a bad one by looking. Here the number is all there is, and whether the number is measuring anything you care about is the question the rest of this set is about.

5Twelve attributes, and no picture at all

Where the teapot stops helping

A teapot has three dimensions because space does. An attribute set has as many dimensions as there are attributes, and twelve is an ordinary number for a real interpretation. Every trace is then a point with twelve coordinates, in a space that cannot be drawn, turned, or looked at from anywhere.

Three things stop working and one thing keeps working, and sorting out which is which is most of what this module set does.

  • Turning it by hand stops working. A direction in three dimensions takes two angles. In twelve it takes eleven, and trying them at one-degree spacing would mean about 6×10²⁴ directions. There is arithmetic that returns the answer directly and it does not care how many dimensions there are, which is why everything from module 04 onward uses it.
  • Recognizing a good view stops working. There is no shape to see. A criterion has to stand in for the judgment, and choosing that criterion is the difference between the two methods in the title of this set.
  • Getting away with keeping two stops working. The teapot's best two directions held 81% of the spread, and three attributes gave 94%. Twelve attributes here need more: the best three hold 74.3%, and a display has only three color channels.
  • Everything else keeps working. A direction is still a recipe mixing the originals. A projection is still a weighted sum. Spread is still the thing being measured. All the arithmetic in this set is the arithmetic on this page, applied where you cannot look.

The panel below is the twelve-attribute correlation matrix for this survey. It is what replaces the picture: sixty-six pairs, five of them above 0.7, which says the twelve attributes are nothing like twelve separate pieces of information.

Why it matters

What this buys you before anything technical

Every term in the rest of this set has a teapot version, and holding onto it makes the technical version much easier to keep straight.

  • A feature is an axis. x, y and z for the teapot. An attribute for a survey. The word means the same thing in both.
  • A sample is a point. One point on the teapot's surface, one trace in the survey.
  • A projection is a view. Choosing what to keep and what to throw away.
  • A component is a recipe. So much of this axis, so much of that one. The numbers in the recipe are called loadings and they are what a component is.
  • Variance is how spread out the picture is. The thing being maximized when you turn a teapot to see more of it.

The one place the teapot misleads. A teapot's best view is obviously its best view, because you know what a teapot looks like. That coincidence does not survive the move to attributes, and most of the difficulty in the rest of this set comes from it. Module 05 finds a component holding half the variance that shows a channel worst of any of them, and module 06 measures how far the useful direction sits from the one the arithmetic returns.

If the rest of the set stops making sense at any point, this is the page to come back to. The arithmetic never becomes anything other than: choose a direction, project onto it, measure what you get.

Exercises

  1. Find a view where the teapot stops looking like a teapot. Why: a projection throws a dimension away, and it is worth seeing how much can go with it. Use the two sliders to find a view where you could not tell someone what the object is, then read the spread.
    HintLooking almost straight down the long direction of the pot collapses the spout into the body and gives spreads near 0.45. The front view, at 0.454, is close to the worst the object offers. The three axis views come out at 0.454, 0.573 and 0.618 and the widest available is 0.666, so the range across every possible view is not enormous — which is worth noticing, because the difference in how recognizable the pictures are is much larger than the difference in the number.
  2. Beat the axis views by hand, then check the answer. Why: this is exactly what module 01 does with two attributes and what every method in the set does with many. In step 2, hunt for the widest view with the sliders, write down your two angles and your spread, then press "The widest".
    HintThe arithmetic gives about −61 degrees around and 33 degrees over, with a spread of 0.666. The best axis view, from the top, gives 0.618, so the gain is 7.8%. Most people get within a few hundredths by hand in under a minute, which is the point: the arithmetic in module 04 is a shortcut to an answer you could have found yourself, not a different kind of answer.
  3. Read the recipe. Why: a component is its loadings, and this is the least intimidating place to meet them. In step 3, read how much of x, y and z goes into each of the two picture axes.
    HintAcross is 0.840x + 0.533y − 0.097z and up is 0.359x − 0.412y + 0.837z. Every one of the six numbers is non-zero, so both picture axes are mixtures of all three original ones. Notice also that each recipe's numbers squared add to one — 0.840² + 0.533² + 0.097² = 1.000 — which is what makes it a direction rather than a direction and a stretch.
  4. Do it again with attributes and see what is missing. Why: the arithmetic transfers perfectly and the judgment does not. In step 4, turn the attribute cloud with the same sliders, then jump to the widest view.
    HintThe widest view holds 93.9% of the spread against 76.9%, 54.4% and 68.7% for the three axis views, so the gain is much larger than it was for the teapot. Its horizontal axis is 0.393 RMS + 0.735 envelope + 0.553 peak amplitude, a mixture of all three. What is missing is any way to tell whether that view is good. With the teapot you could see it. Here the number went up and nothing on the screen says whether anything geological came with it.
  5. Add attributes one at a time. Why: this is the situation the whole set exists for, and it is worth seeing it fill in. In step 5, walk the attribute count from 2 to 12 and watch the correlation matrix.
    HintAt twelve attributes there are sixty-six pairs and five of them are above 0.7 in absolute value, which means several attributes are close to restatements of others. The first three directions hold 74.3% of the spread between them, so nine of the twelve dimensions are carrying the last quarter. A display has three color channels. Those two facts together — twelve inputs, three channels, and a great deal of repetition among the twelve — are the practical problem, and every module from here on is some part of solving it.

If you remember nothing else

  • A view is a projection. Keep two directions, throw the rest away. That is all any picture of a higher-dimensional thing ever is.
  • The good direction is a mixture of the axes you were given. The teapot's best view is 0.840x + 0.533y − 0.097z across and 0.359x − 0.412y + 0.837z up. Not x, not y, not z.
  • "How much you can see" is measurable. Project every point and add up how spread out the picture is. Turning the teapot to see more of it is maximizing that number.
  • The arithmetic transfers to attributes exactly. Three attributes behave like three axes, and the best view is again a mixture: 0.393 RMS + 0.735 envelope + 0.553 peak.
  • The judgment does not transfer. You know what a teapot should look like. Nobody knows what an attribute cloud should look like, so a number has to stand in for the judgment.
  • Twelve dimensions cannot be turned by hand. Eleven angles, about 6×10²⁴ directions at one-degree spacing. The arithmetic does not care; you cannot look.

Method

What is computed, and what is left out

The teapot is built from equations in assets/shape3d.js rather than loaded from a model file: the body and lid are surfaces of revolution, the spout and handle are tubes swept along curves, and points are placed on those surfaces with a small deterministic jitter so the result reads as a sampled surface. The default density gives about three thousand points. It is a teapot in the tradition of the Utah teapot used in computer graphics since 1975, and it is not that model.

The tilt. The teapot is rotated by a fixed 34, 27 and 19 degrees about the three axes before anything is measured. Without it the spout and handle lie in one coordinate plane, that plane is the plane of greatest spread, and the best view comes out as exactly the side view — which would teach that the answer is an axis after all. Attribute spaces are arbitrary coordinate systems in the same way, so the general orientation is the honest case.

A view is two perpendicular unit directions: one becomes the horizontal axis of the picture, one the vertical, and everything along the third is discarded. The spread quoted throughout is the variance of the projected points along the horizontal plus the variance along the vertical, which is the quantity that later modules call the retained variance.

The widest view is the first two eigenvectors of the point cloud's covariance matrix, from the same solver every other module in this set uses. That is principal component analysis, applied to three coordinates instead of to attributes, and nothing about it is special to the fact that the three coordinates happen to be positions in space.

The attribute cloud in step 4 uses RMS amplitude, peak envelope and peak absolute amplitude, computed over samples 22 to 50 of a 72 by 72 trace synthetic survey which is described in module 01's Method tab. All three are amplitude measurements in the same units, which is what makes them drawable on axes that share a scale; module 03 is about what happens when they are not. The twelve-attribute matrix in step 5 adds mean absolute amplitude, sweetness, peak frequency, spectral bandwidth, coherence, an amplitude gradient, two texture measures, and RMS amplitude measured on a window above the target.

Simplifications, stated plainly. The teapot is drawn as a point cloud with depth shading and no hidden-surface removal, so points on the far side show through; a solid rendering would look better and would hide that a projection keeps every point. The comparison between the teapot and the attributes is an analogy and it breaks in the way the Why it matters tab describes. Nothing in this module is standardized or normalized, because both clouds are already in comparable units.
