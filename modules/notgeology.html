Components are not geology

The last module, and the one that says what none of this can do. A component is a direction in a space of numbers that satisfies a statistical criterion. Whether it corresponds to a body of rock is a different question, and nothing in the arithmetic is capable of answering it. Four things go wrong here, all of them on the same survey the set has used throughout, and the fourth one is about these modules rather than about the methods.

2The acquisition came back as component four

Ranked above two real ones

Component 4 holds 7.0% of the variance and is the acquisition footprint. It separates neither channel, and it is ranked above components 5 and 6, which do carry some geology.

The longer version

The footprint was put into the survey as a regular striping on a six-trace period, the kind a marine acquisition geometry leaves behind. It enters the attribute set mainly through peak envelope, which carries 0.24 of it, and RMS amplitude at 0.12.

It is periodic, strong and consistent across the whole survey, which makes it exactly the kind of thing a variance-maximizing method collects. Module 07 noted that this component has an excess kurtosis of −0.12, which is almost exactly Gaussian, and module 08 noted that the corresponding ICA component converged in two iterations and carries 0.293 of the footprint. Both methods found it, ranked it, and handed it over looking like a result.

The one thing that would have caught it is knowing the acquisition geometry, which is not in the data and is not something either method could have been told.

And the method is not the problem

The best anything manages on the older channel is 1.44 — and that is a raw attribute, beating both methods. The best available is 1.73.

Compare the younger channel in the same six attributes: best available 9.34, ICA 6.30.

The longer version

Module 06 computed, with the channel positions in hand, the best direction that exists anywhere in this attribute space for each target. For the younger channel it reaches 9.34. For the older one it reaches 1.73, and the best principal component gets 1.14, the best independent component 1.40, and the best single raw attribute 1.44.

Everything is close to the ceiling. That is the important part. When every method lands near the best available, the shortfall is not in the methods — the six attributes simply do not carry much about that channel. It is thinner, at a different level, and partly overprinted where the younger system crosses it.

No amount of dimension reduction recovers information that was not in the inputs. The correct response to this picture is to go back and compute attributes that would see a thinner bed, not to try a different algorithm.

4An attribute that missed the target took second place

Switch it in and out

Add an attribute computed on a window above the channel system — correct arithmetic, wrong interval — and it becomes the largest loading on component 3, at 0.867.

The longer version

The seventh attribute is RMS amplitude on samples 2 to 16, which sits entirely above the target. It averages 0.04510 inside the younger channel and 0.04604 in the background, a difference of two percent, against 0.08002 and 0.05501 for the same attribute measured on the target itself. It carries almost nothing about the channel.

Module 03 showed why standardizing guarantees it a hearing: every attribute is given a variance of one, which is a statement that they all deserve equal weight. Once that is granted, an attribute carrying nothing still occupies a full dimension, and it can occupy a high-ranked one. The first component falls from 52.62% to 45.10% and the components needed for ninety percent go from four to five.

Nothing in the output flags it. The component looks like any other component, and the only way to notice is to read the loadings and recognize that the largest one belongs to an attribute that should not have been in the list.

The attribute set

5Every score in this module set was cheating

Read this one

Eleven modules have quoted a number called the channel separation, and have used it to say which component was good. That number was computed from the model's own record of where the channels are.

No survey supplies that. Every one of those judgments was made with the answer in hand, by something that was not the method being judged.

What to do instead, on real data

Four substitutes, none as good, all available:

A well. The nearest thing to a ground truth in practice. A component that separates facies at a well is evidence about the component, at one location.

Repeatability. Module 09's test. Re-run from a different start, or on a different part of the survey, and see whether the same components come back. It needs no truth and it catches the failure that looking cannot.

The loadings. Reading what a component is made of takes seconds and catches an attribute that should not be there, as step 4 shows.

Knowing the acquisition. The footprint in step 2 is invisible to every statistic in this set and obvious to anybody who knows the geometry.

The honest position is that dimension reduction reorganizes what you gave it, ranks the result by a criterion of its own, and hands it over without comment. Deciding what any of it means is interpretation, and it stays interpretation however the volumes were produced.

Where to go from here

Everything in this set was a straight line through the data

Show

Every method in this set draws straight lines through the data: a component is a weighted sum of the inputs, with one set of weights for the whole survey. Where the relationship between attributes bends, as module 02's tuning curve did, no weighted sum describes it, and that is where self-organizing maps, generative topographic mapping and the rest of the nonlinear family begin.

Why it matters

The last thing the set has to say

A component volume arrives looking exactly like an attribute volume. It has a name, a number, a colour bar and a rank, and none of those carry any information about whether it corresponds to rock.

  • Read the loadings before the map. It takes seconds, it names the component, and it catches inputs that should not have been in the list.
  • Know what artifacts your survey has. A periodic footprint is strong, consistent and exactly what a variance-maximizing method collects. It will be ranked.
  • When every method lands near the ceiling, stop changing methods. Go back and compute attributes that can see the thing you are looking for.
  • Distrust a good-looking result most of all. Modules 09 and this one both produce clean, convincing, structured maps of nothing in particular.

The two things worth carrying out of the whole set. First, a component is a weighted sum of your inputs, and reading the weights tells you what it is. Second, every method here optimizes something — spread, or non-Gaussianity — and neither of those is "shows me the reservoir". The gap between what was optimized and what you wanted is where the interpretation lives, and it does not close.

That is the end of the set. If one sentence survives: the method found a direction, not a rock.

The two that are not geology are the largest and the fourth largest, so neither the rank nor the appearance helped.
  2. Track the artifact through both methods. Why: it is not a PCA problem, and it is worth confirming that rather than assuming it. In step 2, read the footprint carried by each component under PCA, then switch to ICA.
    HintUnder PCA it concentrates in component 4 at 0.213, which holds 7.0% of the variance. Under ICA it concentrates in component 6 at 0.293, more strongly still, and module 08 found that component converged in two iterations because it is nearly Gaussian. Both methods found the footprint, ranked it among their outputs and handed it over. It entered the attribute set mainly through peak envelope, which carries 0.24 of it, and RMS amplitude at 0.12 — so the place to have caught it was the input attributes. Note that it is only identifiable as footprint here because the striping was put into the survey deliberately and its period is known. On a real survey a periodic map could be acquisition, or a genuine periodic feature such as a dune field or a regular fracture set, and telling them apart needs the survey geometry rather than the data.
  3. Find the case where changing method cannot help. Why: knowing when to stop trying algorithms is worth more than knowing another algorithm. In step 3, compare the younger and older channels: the ceiling, the best component of each method, and the best raw attribute.
    HintYounger channel: best available 9.34, best principal component 3.11, best independent component 6.30. There is a large gap between the methods and the ceiling, so method choice matters. Older channel: best available 1.73, best principal component 1.14, best independent component 1.40, best raw attribute 1.44. Everything is crowded against a low ceiling, which says the six attributes carry very little about that channel. A third method would land in the same place. The useful response is to compute attributes that can see a thinner bed at a different level, which is a decision about the inputs and not about the algorithm.
  4. Put a useless attribute in and find it from the output alone. Why: this is the check that catches it, and it is the only one available. In step 4, switch the seventh attribute in, then try to identify it from the loadings without looking at which one you added.
    HintIt is the largest loading on component 3, at 0.867 — nearly twice what any other attribute carries there, and its own largest loading as well. Module 03 reads it the same way. So it is spread across two high-ranked components and dominates one of them. Adding it drops the first component from 52.62% to 45.10% and pushes the components needed for ninety percent from four to five. Nothing about the maps says anything is wrong. Reading the loadings and asking "what is that attribute doing there" is the whole of the defense.
  5. Work out what you would actually use on a real survey. Why: every score in this set came from the model, and you will not have one. Read step 5, then list the checks you could run on a survey with no ground truth.
    HintFour, none as good as the truth and all available: a well, which gives evidence at one location; repeatability, from module 09, which catches components the data did not determine and needs no truth at all; reading the loadings, which catches inputs that should not be in the list and takes seconds; and knowing the acquisition geometry, which is the only thing that would have caught the footprint in step 2. Notice what they have in common — none of them is a statistic computed from the components, and every one brings in information from outside the analysis.

If you remember nothing else

  • A component is a direction that satisfied a criterion. Whether it is a rock is a separate question no statistic on the page can answer.
  • An artifact will be ranked. The acquisition footprint came back as component 4 under PCA and component 6 under ICA, looking like a clean result in both.
  • The largest component is often nothing. Here it holds 52.6% of the variance and separates the channel at 0.22.
  • When everything lands near the ceiling, the inputs are the problem. On the older channel: best available 1.73, best method 1.40, best raw attribute 1.44.
  • An attribute on the wrong interval takes a high rank. 0.867 on component 3, and nothing in the output flags it.
  • Every score in this set was computed from the answer. On real data you have a well, repeatability, the loadings, and knowledge of the acquisition. Use all four.

Method

What is computed, and what is left out

The survey, the six attributes, the seventh, the standardization, the separation statistic and the footprint statistic are all exactly as in modules 03 to 08, so every number here can be set beside the ones there. The references of 9.342 and 1.727 are module 06's supervised discriminant and are described in its Method tab.

The footprint statistic is the projection of a map onto the six-trace striping that was put into the survey, as a fraction of that map's own standard deviation. It is only computable because the striping is known exactly, which is the whole point of step 2: on a real survey the equivalent knowledge comes from the acquisition geometry rather than from any measurement of the data.

The admission in step 5 is not a rhetorical device. Every figure in this set called a channel separation was computed by comparing traces the model marks as channel against traces the model marks as margin. That information does not exist for a real survey. It was used throughout because a teaching set needs a way to say "this component is better than that one" and an interpreter's judgment cannot be plotted. The substitutes listed in step 5 are what remains when it is taken away, and they are genuinely weaker.

Simplifications, stated plainly. One synthetic survey, one window, two geological targets and one artifact. A real survey has more of all four and they are not cleanly separable into a list. The footprint is perfectly periodic and real footprint is not. The channel positions are known exactly and on real data they would themselves be interpretations with their own uncertainty, which would widen every number quoted here.

Where this departs from production software. Nowhere in the arithmetic. The diagnostics assembled on this page — the footprint projection, the separation against a known target, the comparison against a supervised ceiling — are not things a package computes, because they need information the package does not have.
