Module 02  ·  Dimension reduction

Spread, and the shape of the cloud

Module 01 found the tilt of a cloud by dragging a line through it. That tilt can be written as a single number, and for a dozen attributes as a small square table. These five steps build that number from the points themselves, show what happens to it when the units change, and end on two situations where it describes the data correctly and describes the geology wrongly.

1Where the number comes from

Four quadrants, two signs

Why this step is here. Covariance is usually introduced as a formula. It is easier to see as a count: how many traces sit on the same side of both averages, against how many sit on opposite sides.

Take one trace. Subtract the survey average from its value in the first attribute, and subtract the survey average from its value in the second. Multiply those two deviations together. If the trace is above average in both, the product is positive. If it is below average in both, both deviations are negative and the product is positive again. If it is above average in one and below in the other, the product is negative.

Covariance is the average of that product over every trace. The panel below sorts the traces into the four quadrants and shows what each contributes. A cloud that leans one way has most of its traces in the two positive quadrants; a round cloud has them spread evenly and the positive and negative contributions cancel.

What each quadrant contributes

Positive in crimson, negative in teal
The two quadrant counts do not have to be equal for the covariance to be positive, and the contributions are not shared out evenly between traces. A trace far from both averages contributes far more than a trace near them, because the product of two large deviations is larger than the product of two small ones.

2Multiply one attribute by ten

One number moves, the other does not

Covariance carries the units of both attributes multiplied together. Amplitude times amplitude, or amplitude times hertz. That makes its size depend on how the attributes happen to have been written out, and two volumes describing identical rocks in different units give different covariances.

Correlation removes that dependence by dividing the covariance by both standard deviations before reporting it. What is left is a pure number between minus one and one. The two panels below share a single horizontal axis of gain, so the difference between the two behaviors can be read straight down.

Covariance and correlation as the gain changes

One shared gain axis, both fixed
The covariance curve is drawn against its value at a gain of one, because its actual size runs over several decades across this slider and no fixed axis would hold it. The correlation curve is drawn against its own scale, which never needs to move.

3The ellipse is the two numbers, drawn

Change the pair and watch it thin

Two variances and one covariance are three numbers, and three numbers specify an ellipse. Drawn onto the cloud it encloses the traces within a chosen number of standard deviations, and its shape says the same thing the correlation does: a nearly circular ellipse means the two attributes are close to unrelated, and a thin one means they are close to being the same measurement twice.

The ratio of the long axis to the short one is fixed by the correlation alone when both axes are drawn in standard deviations, which is how this panel is scaled. That is a different picture from module 01, where both axes were in the same amplitude units and the angle of the long axis was the quantity being hunted. Neither picture is more correct; they answer different questions, and which one is on screen has to be known before an angle read off it means anything.

4Eleven attributes, fifty-five pairs

Raise the threshold and watch what survives

With more than two attributes the same calculation is done for every pair and collected into a square table. The diagonal is each attribute with itself, which is one by construction. Everything off the diagonal is a pair, and the table is symmetric, so half of it repeats the other half.

Read as an image, the table is the fastest way to see redundancy in an attribute set before any method is run on it. Raising the threshold below hides the weak pairs and leaves the groups: attributes that are measuring nearly the same thing cluster into blocks that stay dark while everything around them fades.

The correlation matrix

Crimson positive, teal negative
Nothing here is ordered by anything but the list the attributes were computed in. Software that groups a correlation matrix into blocks has reordered the rows and columns to do it, which changes the picture and none of the numbers.

5Two ways a correlation misleads

Switch between them below

Correlation measures how well a straight line through the cloud describes it. Where the relationship is not a straight line, or where the cloud is really two clouds, the number comes back looking perfectly respectable and means something other than what it appears to.

Why it matters

Where this shows up in real work

The covariance matrix is the input to principal component analysis, and everything PCA can see about a dataset it sees through that matrix. Knowing what the matrix keeps and what it throws away is knowing in advance what the method can and cannot find.

  • Choosing an attribute set. Adding a tenth attribute that correlates at 0.95 with one already in the set adds a dimension and almost no information. The matrix says so before anything is run.
  • Reading someone else's crossplot. A tilt on screen is a tilt in whatever units the axes were drawn in. Two figures of the same two attributes can lean at different angles and both be correct.
  • Quoting a correlation in a report. A single number describes a straight line through one population. Where a survey contains two facies, the number can be produced almost entirely by the difference between them.
  • Anticipating what PCA will do. Second-order statistics are all that PCA has. Anything that does not show up in a covariance matrix will not show up in a principal component either, which is the opening that module 07 walks through.

Two points that are commonly confused. The first is treating a low correlation as evidence that two attributes are unrelated. It is evidence that no straight line relates them, and step 5 shows a strong, well understood physical relationship reported at a number that hides its own reversal. The second is treating a high correlation as evidence that one attribute causes or predicts the other. Both may be responding to a third thing neither of them measures.

Every number in this module is descriptive. None of it decides anything, and the decisions start in module 03, where the same data give two different answers depending on a choice that is easy to make without noticing.

Exercises

  1. Take one attribute out of its units. Why: this is the difference that decides whether a method is run on a covariance matrix or a correlation matrix, which is the whole of module 03. With RMS amplitude and peak envelope selected, move the gain slider from 1× to 10× and read the covariance and the correlation at each stop. Before starting: if the second attribute is multiplied by ten, by what factor does the covariance change? Commit to a number.
    HintCovariance goes from 95.6 to 956.4, a factor of ten, because it carries one power of the second attribute's units. The variance of that attribute goes from 206.5 to 20645.3, a factor of a hundred, because it carries two. The correlation sits at 0.6502 at every stop, to four figures, because the division by both standard deviations cancels the gain exactly. Nothing about the rocks changed at any point in that slide.
  2. Read the covariance out of the quadrants. Why: a covariance is an average of products, and seeing which traces dominate it explains why a few extreme values can carry it. In step 1, with RMS amplitude and peak envelope, read the four quadrant contributions and the trace counts.
    HintAbove average in both: 1701 traces contributing 58.36. Below average in both: 2156 traces contributing 47.90. The two mixed quadrants hold 916 and 411 traces and contribute −1.44 and −9.18. The four add to 95.64, which is the covariance. Notice that the larger of the two positive quadrants by trace count is not the larger by contribution: 2156 traces below average in both contribute less than 1701 traces above average in both, because the bright traces sit further from the averages and a product of two large deviations outweighs a product of two small ones.
  3. Find the redundant group. Why: this is the check worth running before a dimension reduction, not after it. In step 4, raise the threshold from 0 to 0.70 and note which pairs are still visible, then take it one stop further to 0.75.
    HintFive of the fifty-five pairs survive at 0.70, and only three of them survive at 0.75. The five are: RMS with mean absolute amplitude at 0.963, coherence with the amplitude gradient at −0.793, peak absolute amplitude with peak envelope at 0.787, GLCM contrast with GLCM homogeneity at −0.738, and coherence with GLCM contrast at −0.715. The first is the clearest case for dropping something: RMS and mean absolute amplitude are two summaries of the same window that differ only in whether the values are squared first, and carrying both spends a dimension on almost nothing. Lowering the threshold to 0.60 brings back twelve pairs rather than five, which is the more honest picture of this attribute set: redundancy is a continuum and the threshold is a line drawn across it. The negative pairs are just as redundant as the positive ones — a correlation of −0.79 predicts one attribute from the other exactly as well as +0.79 does, and only the sign of the slope differs.
  4. Walk a correlation across a tuning thickness. Why: amplitude against bed thickness is the best known non-monotonic relationship in seismic interpretation, and a correlation coefficient cannot represent it. In step 5 with the bend case selected, read the correlation for the whole thickness range, then set the range to 2–13 ms, then to 13–26 ms. Before looking: the physical relationship is the same in all three. Will the three numbers agree?
    HintOver the whole range the correlation is −0.667. Below 13 ms it is +0.866 and above 13 ms it is −0.777. The same 556 traces, the same physics, and the sign of the answer depends on where the range was cut. The binned mean rises from 71.4 at the thin end to a maximum of 104.4 at 12.7 ms and falls back to about 74 at 26 ms, which is the tuning curve: below the tuning thickness the top and base reflections reinforce as the bed thickens, and above it they separate. A correlation coefficient fits one straight line through both limbs and reports the average slope, which describes neither.
  5. Split a correlation by facies. Why: a survey is rarely one population, and a correlation computed across two of them can belong entirely to the difference between them. In step 5 with the two-populations case selected, read the correlation for all traces, for background traces only, and for young-channel traces only. Do it for three pairs: RMS amplitude against coherence, peak envelope against coherence, and RMS amplitude against GLCM contrast.
    HintThe three go wrong in three different directions. RMS against coherence reads −0.622 over the whole survey, +0.005 in the background alone and −0.607 inside the channel: within the background there is no relationship at all, and the survey-wide number is produced by the channel traces being both brighter and less coherent than everything around them — a statement about where the channel is, not about how two attributes relate. Peak envelope against coherence goes the other way, −0.228 overall understating a real −0.558 inside the channel, because three and a half thousand background traces dilute it. RMS against GLCM contrast changes sign: +0.534 across the survey, +0.254 in the background, −0.102 inside the channel. All three are the same trap. Knowing how many populations are inside the window is a precondition for quoting any correlation computed across it.

If you remember nothing else

  • Covariance is the average product of two deviations. Positive when traces sit on the same side of both averages, negative when they sit on opposite sides.
  • Covariance carries units and correlation does not. Rescaling an attribute changes every covariance it appears in and changes no correlation.
  • The covariance matrix is the whole of what PCA sees. Anything absent from it is absent from every principal component that follows.
  • A tilt on a crossplot depends on the axis scaling. In standard deviations the shape is set by the correlation; in matched physical units the angle is what module 01 measured. Know which plot is in front of you.
  • Correlation is a straight line fitted to the cloud. Where the relationship bends, the number can take the sign of neither limb, as amplitude against bed thickness does.
  • A correlation across two populations can belong to the difference between them. Split by facies before quoting one, or the number describes where the channel is rather than how the attributes relate.

Method

What is computed, and what is left out

The survey is the same one module 01 uses, built by assets/attrfield.js: 72 by 72 traces at 2 ms, a younger sinuous channel, an older and thinner channel at a different orientation, a levee wedge, background interbeds, a 30 Hz Ricker wavelet, acquisition footprint on a six-trace period and 12% random noise.

The eleven attributes are all computed over samples 22 to 50 of each trace. RMS amplitude, mean absolute amplitude and peak absolute amplitude are the three obvious summaries of the window. Peak envelope is the largest value of the envelope, from the analytic trace. Sweetness is peak envelope divided by the square root of peak frequency. Peak frequency and spectral bandwidth are the mode and the amplitude-weighted standard deviation of the window's magnitude spectrum. Coherence is the semblance of a three-by-three trace group. The amplitude gradient is a Sobel stencil on the RMS map. GLCM contrast and homogeneity are computed on a sixteen-level quantization of the RMS map in a seven-by-seven window, following Haralick et al. (1973). All amplitude-valued attributes are multiplied by 1000 for display, which cancels out of every correlation.

The crossplot axes are scaled independently, each to four standard deviations of its own attribute, so that any pair can be shown whatever units they are in. That is a different convention from module 01, where both axes carried the same amplitude units into a square box so that the angle on screen was the angle in the data. In this module the angle on screen is not a physical quantity, and the readouts give the correlation rather than an angle.

Covariance and variance are computed dividing by the number of traces rather than by one less than it. The difference is one part in five thousand here and is stated so that a reader checking against their own calculation knows which convention is on screen.

Step 5's bed thickness is the true thickness of the younger channel sand in the model, in two-way time, not a thickness estimated from the seismic. The binned mean peaks at 12.7 ms, which is the tuning thickness for a 30 Hz Ricker on this model; the quarter-wavelength rule of thumb would give 8.3 ms, and the two are different conventions rather than a disagreement. Only traces the model marks as young channel are included, which is 556 of the 5184.

Simplifications, stated plainly. The facies labels in the two-populations case come from the model and would have to be interpreted on real data, which is itself a source of error this panel does not show. The noise is Gaussian and independent from trace to trace. The footprint is perfectly periodic. Nothing here uses dip, so the coherence is a lateral waveform similarity with no structural correction, which on dipping data would read low for reasons that have nothing to do with the channel.

Where this departs from production software. Nowhere. Covariance and correlation are computed identically everywhere, and the only choice a package makes that this module also makes is the divisor. Implementations differ in whether they decimate before computing the matrix on a large volume; this one uses every trace.
