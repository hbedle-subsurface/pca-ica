Twenty attributes. Three color channels. Which three?

Dimension reduction is the answer that machine learning gives to that question, and it arrives wrapped in vocabulary nobody defines: feature, sample, loading, score, whitening, embedding, negentropy. None of it is difficult. Most of it is one operation repeated, and that operation can be drawn.

These modules build principal component analysis and independent component analysis from a crossplot upward, on synthetic seismic that you can change while you watch. No prior exposure to either method is assumed, and no matrix appears until after the thing it computes has already been found by hand.

Everything is computed live in your browser from the numbers on screen. Nothing is a stored picture, so the panels can disagree with the textbook version of the same figure — and in a couple of places, they do.

About

Who this is for, and what it costs you

These modules are built for students and for anyone who has been handed a dimension reduction result and asked to interpret it. They assume a geologist's training and no background in statistics or linear algebra. The seismic in them is synthetic and computed in the browser, which means the geology can be changed while a method is running on it.

Nothing you do in a module leaves your browser. There is no server, no account and no upload. A single shared script records that a page was opened, so that the university can see the modules are being used; there is no cookie, no identifier and nothing about you. Every module runs from a local copy with no network at all, and the state of the controls is held in the address bar so that an instructor can send a link that opens an exact configuration.

The arithmetic behind every panel lives in one small file, assets/multivar.js, which is checked against cases with known answers by verify-multivar.js before any teaching text is written on top of it. Twice so far, the measurement has contradicted a draft of the text, and on both occasions the text was what changed.
